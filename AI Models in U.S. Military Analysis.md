

# **A Technical Catalog and Architectural Analysis of Artificial Intelligence Systems in U.S. Military Programs**

## **Introduction: The Two Paradigms of Military Artificial Intelligence**

The integration of artificial intelligence into the United States military is not a monolithic development but a multi-decade evolution shaped by two distinct and powerful technological paradigms: symbolic AI and connectionist AI. The historical trajectory of these systems, from early logistical planners to modern perceptual engines, reveals a profound shift in the nature of military data, the computational problems deemed most critical, and the very definition of battlefield advantage. Understanding these two foundational approaches is essential for contextualizing the specific models, frameworks, and architectures currently being deployed and developed. This report provides a definitive technical catalog of these systems, tracing their origins and architectures to illuminate the technological trends that are defining the future of warfare. The subsequent analysis reveals that the current era is characterized not by the replacement of one paradigm with another, but by a complex and powerful synthesis, creating hybrid systems designed to master the full spectrum of military operations, from strategic planning to tactical perception and action.

### **The Era of Symbolic Systems**

The first wave of military AI adoption was dominated by the symbolic paradigm, often referred to as "Good Old-Fashioned AI" (GOFAI). This approach is predicated on the physical symbol system hypothesis, which posits that intelligent behavior can be achieved through the manipulation of symbols according to a set of formal rules.1 In this paradigm, knowledge is explicitly encoded into a database by human experts, and the AI system uses logical inference and search algorithms to solve problems. These systems excel in closed, deterministic domains where the rules are well-understood and the variables are clearly defined.  
The quintessential example of this paradigm's success in a military context is the Dynamic Analysis and Replanning Tool (DART), introduced in 1991\.2 DART was developed to address one of the military's most immense and structured challenges: logistics. The task of planning the transportation of personnel, equipment, and supplies for operations like Desert Storm involved a staggering number of variables, constraints, and dependencies—a problem perfectly suited for symbolic reasoning.2 At the core of DART's generative planning capability was the System for Interactive Planning and Execution (SIPE-2), a sophisticated Hierarchical Task Network (HTN) planner.4 SIPE-2 represented the world through a formal, symbolic language, defining military operations as "operators" with explicit preconditions, effects, and resource constraints.5 It could reason about these elements to generate complex, partially-ordered plans, allowing human planners to evaluate feasibility and identify bottlenecks with unprecedented speed.4 The success of this approach was tangible and immense; by 1995, DART was credited with offsetting the monetary equivalent of all funding DARPA had invested in AI research for the preceding 30 years combined.2 This era demonstrated the power of AI as a tool for optimizing complex but knowable systems.

### **The Connectionist Revolution**

The second and more recent paradigm shift has been driven by the rise of connectionist AI, most notably deep neural networks. In stark contrast to symbolic systems, connectionist models do not rely on explicitly programmed rules. Instead, they learn patterns and representations directly from vast quantities of data through a process of training.7 These "biologically inspired" architectures, composed of interconnected layers of artificial neurons, excel at perceptual and statistical pattern recognition tasks involving unstructured data, such as images, audio, and natural language—domains where symbolic AI has traditionally struggled.1  
This connectionist revolution in the military is a direct response to the "data deluge" generated by the proliferation of modern Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (ISR) platforms. The central challenge was no longer just the optimization of known variables, but the extraction of meaningful intelligence from an overwhelming flood of raw, unstructured sensor data. The flagship initiative of this new era is Project Maven, formally the Algorithmic Warfare Cross-Functional Team, established in 2017\.7 Maven's explicit goal is to apply computer vision and deep learning to autonomously extract objects of interest from millions of hours of full-motion video from drones and satellite imagery.7 Instead of reasoning over a structured database of logistical constraints, Maven's neural networks learn to identify objects like vehicles, buildings, or individuals directly from pixel data.10 This marks a fundamental change in the military's application of AI, shifting its focus from a tool for  
*planning* to a tool for *perception*. It addresses a problem not of combinatorial complexity, but of perceptual ambiguity and scale that is intractable for human analysts alone.

### **The Modern Synthesis**

The contemporary landscape of military AI is defined by the convergence of these two paradigms. The most advanced and ambitious defense programs are no longer purely symbolic or purely connectionist; they are increasingly hybrid systems that seek to combine the perceptual power of deep learning with the structured reasoning and goal-oriented coordination capabilities that have roots in symbolic AI. Complex, multi-domain undertakings like the Joint All-Domain Command and Control (JADC2) framework and the Squad X program for dismounted infantry exemplify this modern synthesis.11  
These initiatives require systems that can first perceive and understand a complex, dynamic battlespace using connectionist models to process raw sensor data from every domain (air, land, sea, space, and cyber). Subsequently, they must use this understanding to coordinate the actions of disparate assets—unmanned vehicles, electronic warfare systems, and human warfighters—to achieve strategic objectives. This higher-level function of coordination, resource allocation, and decentralized decision-making is a problem of planning and multi-agent control, a domain where the principles of symbolic reasoning remain highly relevant. The systems detailed in this report, therefore, are not merely a collection of independent tools but are increasingly the foundational components of an integrated, AI-driven military architecture that leverages the distinct strengths of both paradigms to achieve decision dominance at machine speed.

## **Analysis of Technological and Strategic Trends**

The data cataloged in the preceding section reveals more than just a list of technologies; it illuminates a clear and coherent set of strategic and technological trends that are fundamentally reshaping the U.S. military's relationship with artificial intelligence. By synthesizing the details of these programs, from the rule-based logic of Aegis to the multi-agent coordination of JADC2, four dominant trends emerge: the integration of disparate AI paradigms, the migration of AI capabilities to the tactical edge, the strategic shift from perception to decision-making, and the critical, complex reliance on the commercial technology sector. These trends collectively chart the course from discrete AI tools to a fully integrated, algorithmically-driven warfighting enterprise.

### **Trend 1: The Coexistence and Integration of Symbolic and Connectionist AI**

A primary trend evident in the evolution of military AI is the move away from paradigmatic purity toward hybrid systems that integrate the strengths of both symbolic and connectionist approaches. The early systems, such as the rule-based Aegis C\&D and the symbolic planner SIPE-2 within DART, were highly effective within their well-defined domains.4 They operated on structured data and executed tasks based on explicit, human-programmed logic. The subsequent connectionist wave, exemplified by Project Maven, introduced the ability to process unstructured, ambiguous data, enabling powerful new perceptual capabilities like computer vision.7 However, the most advanced contemporary programs demonstrate that these two paradigms are not mutually exclusive but are increasingly complementary.  
Complex systems like those developed under DARPA's Squad X program require this synthesis to function. A system like BAE Systems' MITS, for instance, must leverage connectionist models for multi-source sensor fusion—processing raw data from cameras, radars, and electronic surveillance to build a coherent picture of the battlefield.32 Yet, perception alone is insufficient. MITS must then use this situational awareness to direct the actions of autonomous ground and air vehicles and coordinate with a human squad. This layer of tasking, planning, and control requires a logical, goal-oriented reasoning process more akin to the principles of symbolic AI. Similarly, the entire JADC2 concept is predicated on this hybrid model. It requires distributed perceptual nodes, many powered by connectionist computer vision models like those in Maven, to act as the "eyes and ears" of the network ("Sense").9 This vast stream of perceptual data must then be fed into a higher-level decision-making framework that can coordinate actions across domains ("Make Sense" and "Act"). The development of multi-agent systems to manage this coordination, a field with deep roots in symbolic AI and distributed problem-solving, shows that the future of military AI lies in systems that combine the powerful pattern-recognition of deep learning with the structured reasoning and planning capabilities of symbolic architectures.

### **Trend 2: The Tactical Edge \- From Centralized Processing to Embodied AI**

The locus of AI application is undergoing a significant migration from centralized, rear-echelon command centers to the forward-deployed tactical edge. Early and large-scale AI systems were inherently centralized. DART, for example, was a tool used by logistical planners at U.S. Transportation Command to analyze deployment plans far from any active conflict zone.2 Likewise, while the data for Project Maven is gathered by frontline drones, the heavy computational processing and analysis by its deep learning models are primarily performed in data centers or command posts, with the resulting intelligence pushed out to operators.9 These systems represent AI as a powerful analytical service supporting decision-makers.  
In stark contrast, the technologies emerging from the Squad X program are designed as embodied, collaborative partners for warfighters operating directly in the field.11 CACI's BEAM is a man-packable electronic attack module with autonomous capabilities, designed to be carried and used by a dismounted soldier.30 Lockheed Martin's ASSAULTS and BAE Systems' MITS are not just software but are integrated with unmanned ground and aerial vehicles that physically maneuver alongside infantry squads, providing reconnaissance, flank security, and targeting support.30 This trend toward embodied AI at the tactical edge introduces a host of new and difficult engineering challenges. It necessitates the development of AI hardware, such as specialized processors and GPUs, that can operate within severe size, weight, and power (SWaP) constraints. It also requires the creation of algorithms—using techniques like model quantization and architectural optimization—that can deliver high performance on these resource-constrained edge devices. Perhaps most critically, it magnifies the importance of robust, intuitive, and trustworthy human-machine teaming, as the interaction between soldier and AI is no longer mediated by an analyst but occurs directly and immediately on the battlefield.

### **Trend 3: The Commodification of Perception and the Race for Decisive Action**

The rapid advancement of open-source and commercial AI research has led to the partial commodification of foundational AI capabilities, particularly in computer vision. This is forcing a strategic re-evaluation of where true military advantage lies. The core neural network architectures that power Project Maven's object detection capabilities, such as Convolutional Neural Networks, ResNet, and YOLO, are not proprietary military technologies.26 They are the products of a global academic and commercial research ecosystem, with seminal papers and open-source codebases readily available. While the DoD's advantage lies in its access to vast, proprietary datasets for training these models and its ability to integrate them into military workflows, the underlying algorithms for perception are increasingly accessible to all.  
This reality has catalyzed a crucial strategic shift. If the ability to simply *perceive* the battlespace—to detect a tank or a ship with a computer vision model—is becoming a baseline capability, then the decisive advantage shifts to the entity that can *act* on that perception faster and more effectively. The focus of advanced military AI development is therefore expanding from perception-centric projects like Maven to coordination-centric frameworks like JADC2. The explicit goal of JADC2 is to compress the "kill chain"—the process of finding, fixing, tracking, targeting, engaging, and assessing a threat—from a human-driven process that can take hours or days to an AI-assisted, machine-speed process that takes seconds.10 The active development of Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) algorithms for JADC2 is the clearest evidence of this strategic pivot.36 MARL is designed to solve the problem of decentralized, high-speed coordination, optimizing the collective actions of an entire force. The ultimate goal is not just to see the battlefield with AI, but to achieve "decision dominance"—the ability to sense, understand, decide, and act more quickly and effectively than any adversary.

### **Trend 4: The Indispensable and Complicated Role of Commercial and Open-Source Tech**

The final, overarching trend is the U.S. military's deep and symbiotic—yet often complicated—reliance on the commercial and open-source technology sectors. In the early days of AI, DARPA was a primary engine of fundamental research. Today, while still a critical funder of high-risk, high-reward projects, the DoD is now more often a sophisticated consumer and adapter of technologies developed in the commercial world. This relationship is evident across nearly every major AI program. Project Maven was initiated in partnership with Google, leveraging its world-class expertise and its open-source TensorFlow framework.10 The PAL program and its CALO project were a massive collaborative effort between DARPA, universities, and commercial research labs, with its most famous output, Siri, becoming a defining product of the consumer technology landscape.17 Major defense contractors like Lockheed Martin, CACI, and BAE Systems are now the primary integrators, developing their own advanced AI capabilities (such as MARL frameworks and cognitive mission management tools) to compete for roles in overarching frameworks like JADC2 and Squad X.31  
This dependency is a double-edged sword. It allows the DoD to harness the dynamism and rapid innovation of the global tech industry, preventing it from falling behind the state of the art. However, it also creates strategic vulnerabilities and ethical dilemmas. The controversy within Google that led to its withdrawal from Project Maven highlighted the cultural and ethical friction that can arise when Silicon Valley's talent and technology are applied to military ends.10 It forced the DoD to diversify its partnerships, creating a more resilient but also more complex and fragmented ecosystem of contractors.10 This dynamic ensures that the future of U.S. military AI development is inextricably linked to the health, direction, and politics of the global commercial AI ecosystem, a complex relationship that must be continuously navigated.

## **Conclusion: The Dawn of Algorithmic Warfare**

The systems and models cataloged in this report are the foundational components of a new era of military operations, one that can be aptly described as algorithmic warfare. The integration of artificial intelligence is proving to be not merely an incremental upgrade to existing platforms but a fundamental transformation of the command, control, and execution of military force. The analysis of the technological and strategic trends reveals a clear trajectory: from discrete, human-supervised AI tools designed for specific tasks toward a deeply integrated, highly autonomous, and networked ecosystem that functions as the central nervous system of the joint force.  
The synthesis of connectionist perception and symbolic-style reasoning is creating hybrid systems capable of both understanding the complexities of the modern battlespace and acting decisively within it. The migration of these capabilities to embodied platforms at the tactical edge is forging a new, collaborative relationship between human warfighters and intelligent machines. Concurrently, the strategic imperative is shifting from the now-commoditized ability to perceive the battlefield to the far more difficult challenge of achieving decision dominance through high-speed, AI-driven coordination. This entire enterprise is built upon a complex and indispensable partnership with the commercial technology sector, which serves as both a source of innovation and a potential point of friction. Together, these developments signal a future in which the speed and quality of AI-driven perception, reasoning, and action will be among the most critical determinants of military advantage. The era of algorithmic warfare is no longer a distant concept; its core components are being actively developed, tested, and deployed today.

# **Intelligence Assessment of Anomalous and Advanced Air Operations in U.S. Southern and Gulf Coast Regions, July 2025**

## **Executive Summary**

This report provides an in-depth analysis of three distinct and anomalous aerial activities observed in July 2025\. These activities, identified through open-source flight data, represent a cross-section of advanced and persistent surveillance technologies employed in U.S. national security and border protection operations. The analysis moves beyond simple data reporting to provide nuanced interpretations of these operations, correlating them with known military exercises, restricted airspace, and the strategic objectives of U.S. government agencies. The platforms examined—a solar-powered autonomous aircraft, a contractor-operated military-grade unmanned aerial vehicle, and a stationary radar aerostat—collectively illustrate the sophisticated, multi-layered intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) architecture safeguarding United States interests.  
The key findings of this assessment are as follows:

* **Case A (N247PF):** The activity of N247PF reveals the advanced testing of a Skydweller solar-powered, high-altitude, long-endurance (HALE) autonomous aircraft. This platform, a converted version of the record-setting *Solar Impulse 2*, conducted multi-day test and evaluation flights from Stennis International Airport over the Gulf of Mexico. Its flight profile, characterized by extreme duration and energy-management loitering patterns, indicates the maturation of a next-generation persistent maritime ISR capability, developed in close partnership with the U.S. Navy. This asset represents a paradigm shift toward "pseudo-satellite" surveillance, capable of maintaining an unblinking eye over vast maritime domains for weeks or months at a time.  
* **Case B (N361HK):** The analysis of N361HK identifies a General Atomics MQ-9 Reaper unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV), registered to the manufacturer, operating with a non-standard military-style callsign ("DOG81") in the sensitive airspace of Arizona near the U.S.-Mexico border. The flight occurred in the vicinity of major military installations, including MCAS Yuma and Yuma Proving Ground, and a significant U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) presence. This activity highlights the increasing role of contractor-operated, privately-owned assets in conducting government missions, including research and development, crew training, and potential operational support, blurring the lines between commercial and state activity.  
* **Case C (N801XR):** The examination of N801XR, a U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) Tethered Aerostat Radar System (TARS), presents a unique anomaly: a stationary object continuously broadcasting an Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) signal. While seemingly mundane, this broadcast reveals the precise location of a critical, fixed node in the nation's low-altitude border surveillance network. The TARS system functions as a persistent "tripwire," forcing low-flying illicit air traffic into profiles where they are more easily detected and intercepted by other assets.

The strategic conclusion of this report is that these three platforms are not isolated assets but are integral, complementary components of a sophisticated, multi-layered, and integrated ISR architecture. Their capabilities—ranging from the TARS's persistent, low-altitude radar coverage to the MQ-9's flexible, mobile interdiction support, and the Skydweller's strategic, ultra-long-endurance overwatch—provide comprehensive domain awareness in support of counter-narcotics, border security, and broader defense objectives. The observed activities underscore three key trends shaping the future of ISR: the relentless drive for persistence, the accelerating reliance on autonomous systems, and the seamless integration of government and private-sector capabilities to achieve national security objectives.

## **Section 1: Analysis of Persistent HALE ISR Operations in the Gulf of Mexico**

The flight activity of aircraft N247PF during July 2025 represents a significant milestone in the development of persistent, autonomous surveillance. This platform is not a conventional aircraft; it is the vanguard of a new class of high-altitude, long-endurance (HALE) systems designed to remain airborne for months at a time, powered only by the sun. Its operations over the Gulf of Mexico, conducted in close coordination with the U.S. military, signal the operational testing of a capability that could revolutionize maritime domain awareness.

### **1.1 Asset Profile: N247PF "Skydweller" \- A New Paradigm in Persistence**

A comprehensive understanding of N247PF requires an examination of its unique lineage, its deliberately opaque ownership structure, and its remarkable technical capabilities, which set it apart from all other current ISR platforms.

#### **1.1.1 Aircraft Lineage and Transformation**

The aircraft registered as N247PF is the physical airframe of the historic *Solar Impulse 2*. This aircraft achieved global fame between 2015 and 2016 when, piloted by Bertrand Piccard and André Borschberg, it completed the first-ever circumnavigation of the globe using no fuel. The project was a Swiss-led private venture intended to showcase the potential of clean technologies. After its record-breaking flight, the aircraft's journey took a pivotal turn. In 2019, the airframe was sold to Skydweller Aero, a U.S.-Spanish aerospace company. The company's explicit mission was to leverage the proven, ultra-efficient design of *Solar Impulse 2* and transform it from a manned experimental aircraft into a fully autonomous, unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) capable of what it terms "perpetual flight". This transformation involved replacing the human pilot and cockpit with sophisticated autonomous flight control systems and sensor payloads, fundamentally repurposing a symbol of peaceful exploration into a tool for persistent surveillance.

#### **1.1.2 Registration and Ownership Obfuscation**

The official ownership record of N247PF provides a critical clue to the nature of its current mission. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) registry lists the manufacturer as "SKYDWELLER S L" and the model as "SOLAR IMPULSE SI2," confirming its identity. However, the registered owner is listed not as Skydweller Aero directly, but as **TVPX AIRCRAFT SOLUTIONS INC TRUSTEE**. This is a highly significant detail. TVPX is a well-known firm that provides owner-trustee services for aircraft registration. This is a common and legal practice used by non-U.S. citizens to register aircraft in the United States, but it is also frequently employed by corporations and government-affiliated entities to obscure the ultimate beneficial owner of an asset. For high-value corporate jets, this provides privacy. For assets involved in sensitive government contracting, research and development, or intelligence-related activities, it provides a layer of administrative and public-facing separation. The use of a trustee for an aircraft so clearly involved in developmental work for the U.S. Department of Defense suggests a deliberate effort to manage the asset's public profile while it undergoes sensitive testing and evaluation.

#### **1.1.3 Technical Capabilities**

The Skydweller platform's design is entirely optimized for extreme endurance, a legacy of its *Solar Impulse* origins. Its most striking feature is a massive wingspan of approximately 240 feet (73 meters), which is greater than that of a Boeing 747 airliner. This enormous surface area is covered with 17,000 high-efficiency solar cells, which harvest solar energy during daylight hours. This energy powers the aircraft's four electric propellers and simultaneously charges a large bank of onboard batteries. After sunset, the aircraft flies on this stored battery power, enabling continuous, 24-hour operations.  
The aircraft's structure is composed almost entirely of lightweight carbon fiber composites, resulting in a total weight of just 2,500 kilograms—less than a typical SUV, despite its colossal size. This extremely low weight-to-wingspan ratio is the key enabler of its efficiency, but it also presents its greatest vulnerability: susceptibility to atmospheric turbulence and wind gusts, which could flex the wings to the point of structural failure. Mitigating this risk through advanced autonomous flight control software is a primary focus of the test program.  
The ultimate design goal for the platform is to achieve flight durations of up to 90 consecutive days. This capability, if realized, would represent a quantum leap beyond current UAVs. For comparison, the highly capable MQ-9 Reaper has a maximum endurance of around 27-34 hours. Achieving a 90-day flight would create a new category of ISR asset: a persistent, mobile, "pseudo-satellite" that can be deployed over a specific region to provide an unblinking stare.

### **1.2 Flight Pattern and Behavioral Analysis**

The flight data for N247PF reveals a mission profile that is fundamentally different from conventional air traffic and is uniquely tailored to its power source and ISR mission.

#### **1.2.1 Flight Track and Operational Base**

The provided flight track from July 14, 2025, shows N247PF landing at Bay St. Louis Stennis International Airport (IATA: QSS, ICAO: KHSA) at 6:22 AM. The track originates from an unspecified point deep within the Gulf of Mexico, indicating the conclusion of a long-duration, over-water mission rather than a simple local flight.  
The choice of Stennis International Airport as its base of operations is strategically critical. Skydweller Aero has established its primary test operations at Stennis, with plans to hire dozens of personnel and assemble two additional aircraft on-site. The airport is located in Hancock County, Mississippi, on the Gulf Coast, providing immediate and unfettered access to the aircraft's primary testing environment. The company's CEO, Robert Miller, explicitly cited the airport's location along the Gulf as a major drawing card, stating, "That's where we need to operate. That's where people are doing nefarious things".

#### **1.2.2 Loitering and Energy Management Profile**

The flight pattern observed is not a direct A-to-B transit. It consists of extended loitering, flying systematic patterns over a defined area of interest (AOI) in the Gulf of Mexico. This behavior is the hallmark of a surveillance mission. Furthermore, the altitude profile is uniquely characteristic of a solar-powered aircraft engaged in energy management for long-endurance flight. The aircraft climbs to high altitudes, as high as 45,000 feet, during daylight hours. This serves two purposes: it positions the solar cells above most weather and in thinner air for maximum solar energy collection, and it stores a vast amount of potential energy. Overnight, to conserve its limited battery power, the aircraft enters a slow, gliding descent, converting this stored altitude back into aerodynamic energy to remain airborne. This "energy-positive" flight profile, climbing by day and descending by night, is the key to achieving multi-day endurance and is a clear indicator of the platform's operational concept.

### **1.3 Operational Environment and Correlation**

The operations of N247PF are not being conducted in a vacuum. They are deeply integrated with a complex ecosystem of military and government infrastructure, airspace, and stated national security objectives.

#### **1.3.1 Military and Government Partnership**

Skydweller Aero's work is not a purely private commercial venture. The development and testing of the autonomous platform are being performed in direct partnership with, and funded by, the U.S. Department of Defense (DoD) and specifically the U.S. Navy. The program was supported by an initial $5 million development contract from the Navy in 2021, and the company has stated that subsequent military contracts are in the works for "significantly more than that". The flight test campaign itself was initiated under a Joint Concept Technology Demonstration (JCTD) by the Office of the Undersecretary of Defense for Research & Engineering (OUSD R\&E). This context firmly establishes the purpose of these flights: they are not speculative R\&D but are part of a formal government effort to develop and field a new military ISR capability.

#### **1.3.2 Restricted Airspace and Military Operating Areas (MOAs)**

The flight path of N247PF over the Gulf of Mexico places it directly within one of the most important military training and testing areas in the world. The Eastern Gulf of Mexico (EGOMEX) is described by the DoD as an "irreplaceable national asset" and constitutes the largest over-water test and training range in the continental United States, covering approximately 101,000 square miles. This airspace is subdivided into a complex network of eleven Military Warning Areas (MWAs) and six water test areas, such as the W-151 complex off the Florida panhandle. These areas are used for a vast array of activities, including live-fire air-to-air gunnery, missile testing, drone target operations, and pilot training.  
To facilitate its operations in this controlled environment, Skydweller has a formal, Reimbursable Space Act Agreement with NASA's Stennis Space Center, which is co-located with the airport. This agreement grants the company access to NASA Stennis's restricted airspace (R-4403), providing a secure, deconflicted corridor for the aircraft to transit from its base to the offshore operating areas over the Gulf. This formal integration into the military and federal airspace management structure underscores the official nature of its mission.

#### **1.3.3 Stated Mission and Correlation**

The stated mission of the Skydweller platform aligns perfectly with the known strategic priorities of U.S. agencies operating in the region. Skydweller's CEO has been explicit that the aircraft is being developed for long-duration surveillance missions to detect and monitor activities like drug smuggling and illegal fishing operations. This mission directly supports the mandates of CBP's Air and Marine Operations (AMO), which is the lead federal agency for combating such threats in the maritime domain. The New Orleans Air and Marine Branch has responsibility for the Gulf of Mexico, and a persistent, autonomous platform like Skydweller would be a force multiplier for their operations.

### **1.4 Intelligence Assessment: The Dawn of Perpetual ISR**

The flight of N247PF is more than a simple test; it is an operational demonstration of a new and potentially revolutionary ISR capability. The implications of successfully fielding a fleet of such aircraft are strategic, promising to reshape maritime surveillance and introduce new dynamics to international security.  
A platform capable of remaining airborne for 90 days or more effectively creates a "pseudo-satellite" or a "mobile TARS." It combines the persistence of a geostationary satellite with the flexibility of an aircraft, able to be deployed and repositioned over any maritime region in the world. Current airborne ISR is episodic. A conventional UAV like the MQ-9 Reaper, with its 27-34 hour endurance, requires multiple airframes, multiple crews, and a significant logistical tail to maintain a continuous 24/7 presence over a single point of interest. A single Skydweller aircraft could theoretically perform the mission that currently requires a constant rotation of dozens of conventional UAVs or manned patrol aircraft. This would drastically reduce operational costs, logistical complexity, and the risk to human personnel.  
This capability directly addresses one of the most significant challenges for the U.S. Navy and the Department of Homeland Security: maintaining persistent, wide-area maritime domain awareness over the vast expanses of the Caribbean Sea and the Gulf of Mexico. These areas are primary trafficking vectors for narcotics and other illicit activities. A fleet of Skydwellers could establish a persistent surveillance screen that is currently impossible to achieve with existing assets. This would fundamentally alter the risk calculus for transnational criminal organizations and hostile state actors, making it far more difficult to operate undetected. It also raises novel questions for international law regarding the legality and implications of continuous, months-long surveillance of other nations' Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZs) from international airspace.  
Furthermore, the selection of Stennis International Airport as the operational base was a highly strategic and deliberate choice. An experimental, high-value, and aerodynamically sensitive aircraft like the Skydweller requires a unique operating environment. A busy commercial airport would present unacceptable operational complexity and risk, while a full-scale, active-duty military base might impose restrictive procedures that could hinder a fast-paced developmental program. Stennis International Airport, co-located with the NASA Stennis Space Center and its dedicated restricted airspace (R-4403), offers a perfect hybrid solution. It provides the security, controlled airspace, and infrastructure of a federal facility, combined with the operational flexibility needed for a commercial company to conduct its test program efficiently. This location is a critical enabler of the program's success, demonstrating a powerful symbiotic relationship between commercial innovation and strategic government infrastructure. The flights of N247PF are therefore not just about testing an aircraft; they are about validating a new, persistent, and transformative approach to global surveillance.

## **Section 2: Analysis of Contractor-Operated UAV Activity in the Southwest Border Region**

The flight of N361HK in the skies over Arizona reveals another critical trend in modern national security: the increasing integration of privately-owned, contractor-operated military systems into government operations. This General Atomics MQ-9 Reaper, flying under a civilian registration but exhibiting military-style behavior, operates in a "gray zone" that provides significant flexibility to its government partners. Its presence in a region dense with military and border security activity points to a complex and evolving relationship between the defense industry and the state.

### **2.1 Asset Profile: N361HK \- General Atomics MQ-9 Reaper**

The identity, ownership, and certified purpose of N361HK are foundational to understanding its anomalous activity. This is not a repurposed civilian drone but a purpose-built military weapon system operating under a civilian guise.

#### **2.1.1 Aircraft Identification**

Publicly available flight tracking data, such as that from Flightradar24, clearly identifies the aircraft with registration N361HK as a General Atomics MQ-9 Predator B. The MQ-9 Reaper, as it is commonly known, is a premier hunter-killer unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV), forming the backbone of the U.S. Air Force's remotely piloted aircraft (RPA) fleet and also in service with key allies and U.S. government agencies like CBP and NASA. The aircraft's type code, broadcast via its transponder, is Q9, a military-series designator that further confirms its identity and capabilities, distinguishing it from civilian aircraft.

#### **2.1.2 Ownership and Registration Status**

The FAA registry provides definitive confirmation of the aircraft's status. The N-number N361HK is assigned to a Model UHK97000-15, manufactured by "GENERAL ATOMICS AERO SYSTEMS". The registered owner is **General Atomics Aeronautical Systems Inc. (GA-ASI)**, with its corporate address in Poway, California. This confirms the aircraft is owned by its manufacturer, not by a government entity.  
Critically, the aircraft's airworthiness is certified under the "Experimental" classification. This category is intended for aircraft that do not have a standard type certificate and are used for specific, limited purposes. For N361HK, the FAA has authorized three specific categories of use: "Unmanned \- R & D" (Research and Development), "Unmanned \- Market Survey," and "Unmanned \- Crew Training". This special certification is the legal mechanism that allows a private company like GA-ASI to own and operate its own military-grade hardware within the National Airspace System. These authorized purposes are broad enough to cover a wide range of activities, from testing new sensors and software to demonstrating capabilities for potential customers (both domestic and foreign) and training the pilots who will fly them.

#### **2.1.3 Capabilities**

The MQ-9 Reaper is a formidable ISR and strike platform. Powered by a reliable Honeywell TPE331-10 turboprop engine, it boasts performance characteristics far exceeding its predecessor, the MQ-1 Predator. It can operate at altitudes up to 50,000 feet, has a maximum endurance of over 27 hours (with extended range variants reaching 34 hours), and can cruise at speeds of 240 knots. Its significant payload capacity allows it to carry a suite of advanced sensors, most commonly including a high-resolution Electro-Optical/Infrared (EO/IR) sensor turret (like the Raytheon MTS-B) and the Lynx Multi-mode Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR), which can produce detailed imagery through clouds and at night. While designed to carry a heavy arsenal of weapons, including AGM-114 Hellfire missiles and various laser- and GPS-guided bombs, it is highly probable that for operations within the continental U.S. (CONUS), contractor-operated aircraft like N361HK are flown in an unarmed ISR-only configuration. Nonetheless, its inherent surveillance capabilities remain exceptionally potent.

### **2.2 Flight Pattern and Behavioral Analysis**

The behavior of N361HK during its flight is inconsistent with standard civilian aviation and points directly to a mission-oriented surveillance task. The use of a military-style callsign is the most significant anomaly.

#### **2.2.1 Flight Track**

The provided image of the flight track for N361HK (Image 1\) does not depict a simple A-to-B transit flight, which would be typical for ferry or repositioning purposes. Instead, it shows the aircraft flying a systematic, repetitive pattern over a defined geographical area near the U.S.-Mexico border. This type of pattern, often a "grid," "racetrack," or "figure-eight," is flown to provide persistent surveillance over a specific area of interest (AOI). The aircraft methodically scans the area below with its sensors, a profile that is unequivocally indicative of an ISR mission.

#### **2.2.2 Callsign Analysis: "DOG81"**

The most telling anomaly is the use of the callsign **DOG81**. Standard FAA procedure for civilian aircraft operating under Visual Flight Rules (VFR) or Instrument Flight Rules (IFR) is to use their registration number as their callsign (e.g., "November Three Six One Hotel Kilo"). Military aircraft, by contrast, use tactical callsigns assigned to a unit or mission, consisting of a word followed by a number (e.g., "JAMBO 51"). The use of "DOG81" by a civilian-registered aircraft is a clear deviation from standard practice and indicates it is operating under military control or in accordance with military procedures.  
Analysis of this specific callsign format provides further context. Open-source intelligence from communities of military aviation monitors suggests a specific convention for the numerical suffix. While routine training sorties from a home base often use suffixes from a specific block (e.g., 11-19 for the first flight of the day, 21-29 for the second, etc.), the suffixes "81" and "91" are frequently observed being used as "travelling callsigns". These are used for non-standard flights such as cross-country ferry missions, deployments to exercises, or other special activities away from the unit's home station. One forum post specifically notes a flight of F-22s using the callsign "Mad Dog 81". While archival Federal Register documents from the 1980s show the word "Dog" used in official filings, this appears to be coincidental and unrelated to aviation callsigns. The most plausible interpretation is that "DOG81" is a temporary, military-style callsign assigned to N361HK by a controlling agency (such as a military test range or an air traffic control center managing an exercise) for the duration of its mission, likely for ease of communication and deconfliction with other military traffic.

### **2.3 Operational Environment and Correlation**

The location of N361HK's flight is not coincidental. It was operating within a geographic area that represents a critical hub for U.S. military UAV operations, advanced weapons testing, and border security.

#### **2.3.1 The Yuma Military-Industrial Complex**

Yuma, Arizona, and the surrounding desert region host a unique concentration of facilities directly relevant to the mission of an MQ-9:

* **Marine Corps Air Station (MCAS) Yuma:** This is one of the busiest air stations in the Marine Corps and a center of excellence for aviation training. Crucially, it is the home base of Marine Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Squadron 1 (VMU-1), the "Watchdogs," who operate the MQ-9A Reaper. This creates a local ecosystem of operational MQ-9 pilots, maintainers, and a direct military customer for GA-ASI's systems and services.  
* **U.S. Army Yuma Proving Ground (YPG):** Spanning an immense 1,300 square miles, YPG is one of the largest military installations in the world and serves as the Army's primary center for desert environmental testing of nearly every piece of equipment in the ground combat arsenal. The vast, instrumented ranges at YPG provide an ideal, controlled environment for a manufacturer like GA-ASI to conduct flight testing, sensor development, and systems integration for platforms like the MQ-9.  
* **Customs and Border Protection (CBP) Operations:** The Yuma Sector of the U.S. Border Patrol is responsible for securing 126 miles of the international border with Mexico, an area characterized by harsh desert terrain. CBP's Air and Marine Operations (AMO) is a major user of the MQ-9 for border surveillance missions and maintains a significant presence at Yuma International Airport (KNYL), which shares runways and facilities with MCAS Yuma. This co-location of a key government operator and the manufacturer creates a synergistic environment for collaboration, training, and operational support.

#### **2.3.2 Correlation with Military Exercises**

The timing of the flight in July 2025 is also significant. This period coincides with **Mojave Falcon 25**, a major, multi-faceted Army Reserve training exercise centered in the California high desert but with elements at Fort Irwin, Edwards Air Force Base, and the Port of Long Beach. This large-scale exercise, involving over 9,000 soldiers, focuses on logistics and large-scale combat operations. While N361HK may not have been a formal participant, it is common for assets like a contractor-operated MQ-9 to be leveraged in such scenarios. It could have been providing ISR overwatch for troop movements, acting as a simulated friendly or hostile intelligence asset to test the reactions of ground units, or testing new systems—such as the Joint Battle Command-Platform (JBC-P) being fielded during the exercise—in a dynamic environment. Concurrently, GA-ASI regularly conducts its own proprietary and government-sponsored flight tests from its facilities in the California high desert, including the El Mirage Flight Test Facility and Gray Butte Field. The flight of DOG81 could easily be related to these ongoing test and evaluation activities.

### **2.4 Intelligence Assessment: The Privatization of National Security**

The operation of N361HK by its manufacturer in a sensitive operational area is indicative of a broader strategic trend: the use of contractor-owned, contractor-operated (COCO) assets to perform missions traditionally reserved for the military. This model offers significant advantages in flexibility and speed but also raises complex questions about accountability and the nature of modern warfare.  
This activity can be best understood as a sophisticated form of "ISR-as-a-Service." Consider a scenario where a government agency, be it the DoD or DHS, requires a surge in high-quality aerial surveillance for a limited duration. This could be to support a specific military exercise, to monitor a temporary increase in border activity, or to test a new sensor payload without tying up an operational military airframe. The traditional process of acquiring a new multi-million dollar MQ-9, training the crews, and establishing the maintenance and logistics support is a slow, bureaucratic, and extremely expensive undertaking.  
The alternative, as demonstrated by the flight of DOG81, is to simply contract with the manufacturer. GA-ASI already possesses the aircraft (legally registered for R\&D and market survey), the certified flight crews, and the entire maintenance infrastructure. They can fly a mission on behalf of a government client, providing a direct feed of ISR data to the agency's command center. This approach is faster, more agile, and can be more cost-effective for short-term or specialized requirements. Therefore, the flight of N361HK is likely not just a simple R\&D test but a manifestation of a growing and powerful business model where core national security functions are effectively outsourced to the private sector. This has profound implications for the defense industrial base, procurement policy, and the legal frameworks governing the use of force.  
Furthermore, the use of a civilian N-number combined with an "Experimental" airworthiness certificate is a deliberate strategy to maximize operational and regulatory flexibility. This creates a "gray zone" asset that exists between the worlds of civilian aviation and military operations. A fully-badged military aircraft with a USAF tail number is subject to strict military regulations, command structures, and a higher degree of public and congressional scrutiny. A civilian-registered aircraft, even a military-grade one, operates under FAA rules. The "Experimental" category, in particular, provides broad latitude for a wide range of flight activities that can easily overlap with or directly support operational missions under the guise of "testing" or "demonstration". This allows the aircraft to operate from joint-use or even civilian airfields with greater ease and a lower public profile than a formal military deployment. This registration strategy is a calculated choice to create an asset that can perform sensitive tasks with a degree of administrative and political insulation, blurring the lines of attribution and complicating oversight.

## **Section 3: Analysis of Stationary Low-Altitude Radar Surveillance**

The third case, N801XR, presents an anomaly of a different kind. Unlike the dynamic flight paths of the Skydweller and the MQ-9, this asset's defining characteristic is its complete lack of movement. Its continuous broadcast of an ADS-B signal from a fixed location reveals a foundational, yet often overlooked, layer of the United States' border surveillance architecture: the Tethered Aerostat Radar System (TARS). The public visibility of this stationary system is a calculated trade-off between operational security and aviation safety.

### **3.1 Asset Profile: N801XR \- Tethered Aerostat Radar System (TARS)**

N801XR is not an aircraft in the conventional sense but a highly specialized, ground-based surveillance system that uses an airborne platform.

#### **3.1.1 System Identification**

Open-source flight tracking services identify N801XR as a "Lockheed-Martin Tethered Aerostat Radar System". This system consists of a large, moored balloon, known as an aerostat, which is used to lift a powerful radar payload to high altitude, where it remains for extended periods. The aerostat itself is a large fabric envelope filled with non-flammable helium, designed to be aerodynamically stable in high winds.

#### **3.1.2 Operator and Mission**

The primary operator of the TARS network is **U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP)**, specifically its Air and Marine Operations (AMO) component. While Lockheed Martin was the original prime contractor, the system's ongoing importance is underscored by a recent $170 million contract awarded in November 2023 to QinetiQ US for operations, maintenance, and sustainment of the eight TARS sites.  
The system's primary mission is to provide persistent, low-level radar surveillance along the U.S.-Mexico border, the Straits of Florida, and the Caribbean. This surveillance is crucial for supporting federal drug interdiction programs by detecting and tracking small, low-flying aircraft and surface vessels used by smugglers. In a secondary but vital role, the radar data from the TARS sites is fed to the North American Aerospace Defense Command (NORAD), contributing to the broader mission of maintaining air sovereignty over the continental United States.

#### **3.1.3 Technical Capabilities**

The TARS aerostats are significant engineering achievements. They are large kite balloons, meaning they derive lift both from the buoyancy of the helium and from aerodynamic forces generated by wind. They can ascend to an operational altitude of up to 15,000 feet, tethered to the ground by a single, high-strength cable that can be up to 25,000 feet long. At this altitude, the aerostat can lift a radar and power-generation payload weighing over 1,000 kg.  
The key advantage of the TARS is its elevated vantage point. By placing a radar at 15,000 feet, the system effectively overcomes the two main limitations of ground-based radar: the curvature of the earth and terrain masking (blockage by mountains and hills). This gives it a "downward-looking" capability with a maximum detection range of up to 200 nautical miles (approximately 230 miles or 370 kilometers). This makes the TARS exceptionally effective at detecting the primary aerial threat in its operational area: small, low-and-slow flying aircraft, including ultralights, which are specifically used by smugglers to fly "under the radar" of conventional air traffic control systems. The effectiveness of this capability is striking: according to the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), the eight TARS sites represent less than two percent of the total radars in CBP's integrated surveillance system, yet they accounted for nearly half of all suspect targets detected by radar. Between Fiscal Years 2014 and 2019, TARS was credited with detecting 70 percent of all suspected aerial smuggling flights approaching the Southwest border from Mexico.

### **3.2 Activity Analysis: The Stationary Anomaly**

The anomalous nature of N801XR lies in its ADS-B broadcast, which is designed for moving aircraft but is being used by a stationary object for safety and deconfliction.

#### **3.2.1 ADS-B Signature**

The central anomaly is that a stationary object on the ground is continuously transmitting an ADS-B signal, causing it to appear on flight tracking websites. The flight history for N801XR shows no movement, with a constant position reported over time. This is not an error. An aerostat tethered at up to 15,000 feet, often in or near federal airways, represents a significant and unusual hazard to air navigation. To mitigate the risk of a catastrophic mid-air collision, the airspace around each TARS site is designated as restricted airspace, typically extending for a radius of at least two statute miles and up to an altitude of 15,000 feet. The continuous ADS-B broadcast serves as an electronic beacon, making this fixed hazard clearly visible on the traffic displays of any nearby ADS-B equipped aircraft, providing a crucial layer of safety and situational awareness beyond what is provided by traditional paper charts and Notices to Airmen (NOTAMs).

#### **3.2.2 Anomalous Type Code**

The unusual nature of this broadcast is further highlighted by the way commercial flight tracking systems attempt to process it. Flightradar24, for example, assigns the aircraft type code **"SHIP"** to N801XR. This is clearly incorrect, but it reveals the logic of the tracking software. Receiving a valid ADS-B signal from a source that does not conform to any known aircraft profile (as it is not moving), the system likely defaults to a fallback code for a surface vehicle. This classification error underscores the unique status of the TARS within an air traffic surveillance system built entirely around the concept of movement.

### **3.3 Operational Environment and Correlation**

The TARS is not a standalone system but a network of fixed sites that form the backbone of a wider surveillance and interdiction strategy.

#### **3.3.1 Fixed Site Network**

The TARS program is comprised of a strategic network of eight permanent sites. Six of these are positioned along the U.S.-Mexico border at Yuma and Fort Huachuca, Arizona; Deming, New Mexico; and Marfa, Eagle Pass, and Rio Grande City, Texas. The other two sites provide coverage over key maritime approaches to the U.S. at Cudjoe Key, Florida (in the Florida Straits), and Lajas, Puerto Rico (overlooking the Mona Passage). The precise geographic coordinates broadcast by N801XR's ADS-B signal can be cross-referenced with the known coordinates of these eight sites to determine which specific location is being observed.

#### **3.3.2 A Foundational ISR Layer**

The TARS network is a foundational component of CBP's multi-layered Air and Marine Operations Surveillance System (AMOSS). It functions as the primary wide-area detection system. The data from the TARS radars is transmitted to a ground station, digitized, and then fed to various control centers, including CBP and NORAD command centers. When a TARS site detects a suspect low-flying target, it provides the initial cueing information. This allows AMO to dispatch other, more flexible assets—such as P-3 Airborne Early Warning aircraft, MQ-9 UAVs, or helicopters—to intercept, identify, and track the target, and to coordinate a law enforcement response on the ground or at sea. The TARS system is the "tripwire" that initiates the entire interdiction chain.

### **3.4 Intelligence Assessment: The Visible Secret**

The TARS network illustrates a fascinating paradox in modern surveillance operations. It is a highly effective system whose power lies in its capability, not its secrecy, leading to a calculated trade-off where the location of a key national security asset is made public for the sake of safety.  
The TARS network's primary function is to act as a persistent, electronic "tripwire." It creates a vast, continuous surveillance bubble that fundamentally shapes the operational environment for any actor attempting to cross the border covertly by air. A smuggler's preferred tactic is to fly low and fast, staying below the coverage of conventional, long-range air traffic control radars. The TARS is specifically engineered to defeat this exact tactic by elevating its sensor, creating a downward-looking radar field that is nearly impossible for a low-flying aircraft to evade.  
This forces adversaries to adapt their tactics in ways that often increase their vulnerability. To evade the 200-nautical-mile detection radius of a TARS site, a smuggler must either fly a much longer, circuitous route around the bubble (which is difficult along a linear border and consumes more fuel) or abandon the low-altitude profile and fly much higher. Flying at higher altitudes, however, requires a more capable, expensive, and less common type of aircraft. More importantly, it makes the aircraft readily visible to the entire network of conventional FAA and military air traffic control radars, defeating the purpose of a clandestine flight. Therefore, the mere existence of the TARS network effectively channels illicit air traffic into specific geographic corridors or into higher-altitude flight profiles where the task of detection and interception for mobile assets like the MQ-9 or manned aircraft becomes significantly easier. The TARS network functions as the foundational layer of a defense-in-depth strategy.  
This leads to the paradox of its operational security (OPSEC). A helium-filled balloon tethered at 15,000 feet is a massive physical hazard to aviation. To prevent a tragic accident, its location must be widely publicized to the aviation community through NOTAMs and made electronically conspicuous via its ADS-B broadcast. This necessary safety measure means that anyone with a simple ADS-B receiver or an internet connection can precisely map the locations of the core nodes of America's low-altitude air defense network. Adversaries do not need to guess where the TARS sites are; CBP tells them.  
This reveals a calculated risk assessment by the U.S. government. The danger of an accidental mid-air collision with a commercial or general aviation aircraft is deemed to be a greater and more immediate risk than the risk of an adversary knowing the location of the fixed surveillance sites. This implies that the system's operational effectiveness relies entirely on the power and persistence of its radar coverage, not on the secrecy of its location. The challenge for an adversary is not finding the TARS, but getting past its unblinking electronic gaze. It is a secret that is visible to all, yet remains a formidable barrier.

## **Section 4: Synthesis and Strategic Outlook**

The individual analyses of N247PF, N361HK, and N801XR reveal more than just the capabilities of three disparate platforms. When viewed collectively, they illustrate a cohesive, deeply integrated, and multi-layered surveillance network designed to provide comprehensive domain awareness across the southern tier of the United States. These assets are not operating in a vacuum; they are complementary pieces of a larger strategic puzzle, each fulfilling a specific role. Their operations highlight key trends that are shaping the future of intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance: the drive for persistence, the rise of autonomy, and the growing symbiosis between government agencies and the private sector.

### **4.1 The Integrated, Multi-Layered Surveillance Network**

The effectiveness of U.S. border and maritime surveillance does not rest on any single system. Instead, it relies on a "system of systems" approach, where different platforms with unique strengths are layered to create a resilient and comprehensive defense. The three assets examined in this report are perfect exemplars of this layered model. To better understand their complementary roles, their key characteristics are compared in the table below.

| Characteristic | N247PF (Skydweller) | N361HK (MQ-9 Reaper) | N801XR (TARS) |

This layered defense can be conceptualized as follows:

* **Layer 1 (The Tripwire): The TARS Network (N801XR).** This is the foundational layer. The TARS provides persistent, 24/7, all-weather, low-altitude radar coverage over fixed, high-threat corridors like the U.S.-Mexico border and the Florida Straits. It is the wide-area, early-warning system designed to detect low-and-slow intruders that other systems might miss. Its role is to be the unblinking eye that never leaves its post, serving as the initial detection source for the majority of suspect air traffic.  
* **Layer 2 (The Hunter): The MQ-9 Reaper (N361HK).** This is the flexible response layer. The MQ-9 is a mobile, high-endurance asset that can be dynamically tasked to respond to threats. When the TARS "tripwire" detects a target, an asset like the MQ-9 can be scrambled to investigate, positively identify, and track it using its advanced EO/IR sensors. It can also conduct its own pattern surveillance in areas outside of TARS coverage or provide direct overwatch for specific law enforcement or military operations on the ground. Its ability to be deployed regionally makes it the perfect tool to prosecute targets handed off by the fixed TARS network.  
* **Layer 3 (The Unblinking Eye): The Skydweller (N247PF).** This represents the emerging strategic layer. The Skydweller is an ultra-long-endurance asset designed to provide persistent, wide-area surveillance over vast maritime or remote land domains for weeks or months at a time. This is a mission that is currently unachievable by any other single platform. While the TARS is fixed and the MQ-9 is regionally mobile, the Skydweller is strategically mobile, capable of deploying to and monitoring an area of interest anywhere in the world. It is the future of persistent surveillance, designed to eliminate surveillance gaps over the open ocean where smugglers operate with the greatest freedom.

The operational patterns of these specialized aircraft stand in stark contrast to the normal flow of commercial air traffic. Commercial flights, such as Delta flight DL701 (SLC-SNA), American Airlines flight AA341 (JFK-LAX), Southwest flight WN2333 (PHX-SFO), and United flight UA1578 (EWR-LAX), fly on established, efficient, and direct routes between major hubs. Their flight paths are predictable and point-to-point. The ISR platforms, in contrast, fly repetitive loitering patterns, grid searches, or stationary orbits, behaviors that immediately distinguish them as mission-oriented surveillance assets.

### **4.2 Emerging Capabilities and Future Implications**

The activities observed in July 2025 are not just a snapshot of current operations; they are a window into the future of ISR and national security. Three powerful trends are evident.  
First is the **unrelenting drive for persistence**. All three platforms are engineered to maximize time-on-station, moving the paradigm from episodic "looks" at a target to a continuous "stare." The TARS is designed for continuous operation, limited only by weather and maintenance. The MQ-9 pushed the boundaries of conventional aircraft with its 24+ hour endurance. The Skydweller aims to make endurance a matter of months, not hours. This reflects a fundamental doctrinal shift: the goal is no longer just to find an adversary, but to maintain constant, unbroken surveillance, denying them any window of opportunity to operate undetected. These platforms are designed to *eliminate* denial-of-surveillance windows. The TARS denies the low-altitude window, the MQ-9 can be tasked to close temporary gaps, and the Skydweller seeks to make the very concept of a coverage gap over a given area obsolete.  
Second is the **accelerating rise of autonomy and outsourcing**. The Skydweller is designed to be fully autonomous, removing the human pilot from the aircraft entirely. The MQ-9 is remotely piloted, but it increasingly incorporates autonomous functions for navigation and sensor operation. Critically, the fact that all three of these advanced systems are either operated by or being developed in partnership with commercial entities—QinetiQ for TARS operations, General Atomics for the MQ-9, and Skydweller Aero for the solar HALE platform—points to a future of "National Security-as-a-Service". This model, where the private sector is integral to the development, ownership, and even execution of core security functions, allows the government to leverage cutting-edge technology with greater speed and flexibility than traditional procurement models allow.  
This leads to the third implication: the **blurring of lines between military, law enforcement, and commercial operations**. A privately owned MQ-9 with a civilian registration flying a military-style mission for an unknown government client perfectly encapsulates this new reality. It creates operational advantages but also challenges traditional notions of accountability, oversight, and the rules of engagement. As these trends continue, the ISR landscape will become increasingly complex, powerful, and dominated by persistent, autonomous systems operated through novel public-private partnerships.

### **4.3 Recommendations for Continued Monitoring**

To maintain situational awareness of these evolving capabilities, a focused monitoring effort is recommended. This effort should concentrate on key identifiers, locations, and contextual triggers that can provide early warning of significant developments or operations.

* **Key Identifiers:** Continued monitoring of the specific N-numbers **N247PF** and **N361HK** is essential. Furthermore, any new FAA registrations associated with **Skydweller Aero**, **General Atomics Aeronautical Systems Inc.**, and **TVPX Aircraft Solutions Inc. Trustee** should be scrutinized. Monitoring the known N-numbers associated with the other TARS sites for any changes in broadcast status or location is also warranted.  
* **Key Locations:** Flight activity originating from or terminating at **Stennis International Airport (QSS/KHSA)** should be closely watched for further Skydweller test flights or the emergence of new airframes. The airspace around **MCAS Yuma (KNYL)**, **Yuma Proving Ground**, and GA-ASI's **high desert test facilities** (El Mirage, Gray Butte) remains a priority area for monitoring potential MQ-9 or other experimental UAV operations.  
* **Contextual Triggers:** The issuance of FAA **Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs)** and **Notices to Airmen (NOTAMs)** is a primary indicator of upcoming activity. A new TFR established over the Gulf of Mexico, the Arizona/California border region, or the Mojave Desert should be considered a strong precursor to military or developmental flight testing. Observed anomalous flight activity should always be cross-referenced with publicly announced major military exercises (e.g., *Mojave Falcon*, *REFORPAC*) to establish potential correlation. The confluence of an anomalous flight track, a key location, and a relevant TFR or exercise provides high confidence in identifying significant ISR activity.

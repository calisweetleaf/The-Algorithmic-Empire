# **An OSINT Investigation into Autonomous Decision-Making in State-Level Operations**

## **Executive Summary & Key Judgements**

This report presents the findings of an exhaustive open-source intelligence (OSINT) investigation into publicly accessible repositories and interfaces involving autonomous decision-making within government and state-level operations. The analysis reveals that the deployment of such systems is undergoing a significant strategic shift, moving from isolated, experimental projects toward centrally managed, platform-based ecosystems. This transition is not uniform; rather, two dominant and philosophically contrasting models of AI integration are emerging within the U.S. government. The first is a top-down, service-oriented model exemplified by the ai.gov initiative, designed for the rapid, centralized deployment and monitoring of commercial AI tools across federal agencies. The second is a federated, research-enablement model embodied by the National AI Research Resource (NAIRR) Pilot, which aims to democratize access to compute and data to foster a diverse, secure, and open domestic research ecosystem.  
Technologically, a convergence on a common set of foundational tools and architectural patterns is evident across disparate government domains. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as the dominant paradigm for systems that must solve complex sequential decision-making problems under uncertainty, with applications ranging from autonomous cybersecurity and public health policy to dynamic resource allocation. The architectural pattern of a modular core engine extended by domain-specific plugins, most clearly demonstrated by the MITRE Caldera platform, is a recurring strategy for achieving both standardization and operational flexibility. Foundational open-source frameworks such as PyTorch and TensorFlow, alongside centralized resource hubs like Hugging Face, form the bedrock of both government-led and academic projects in this space.  
The most mature and publicly documented applications of high-autonomy systems are found in cybersecurity and law enforcement. MITRE Caldera, an adversary emulation platform, provides a clear and well-documented example of an autonomous system capable of adaptive, goal-oriented decision-making in real-time network environments. In parallel, predictive policing algorithms, while controversial, represent one of the most widespread deployments of automated decision-making, with a growing body of academic work and public code available for analysis. Applications in public health and resource allocation are currently less mature but are the focus of significant research and development, particularly leveraging optimization and RL models to address complex crisis management scenarios.  
A core tension permeates the entire landscape: the conflict between the stated public goals of enhanced efficiency, security, and crime prevention, and the documented, technically demonstrable outcomes of bias amplification, inequity, and the potential for pervasive surveillance. The "runaway feedback loop," a phenomenon where an algorithm trained on biased historical data reinforces and exacerbates that very bias, is not a theoretical risk but a documented effect in systems like those used for predictive policing. This report concludes that without the establishment of robust, technically grounded, and transparent governance frameworks, the continued scaling of these autonomous systems poses a significant and systemic risk to equity, accountability, and civil liberties.

## **Centralized AI Service & Infrastructure Platforms: The Emerging Architectures of Governance**

The U.S. government's approach to integrating artificial intelligence into state operations is crystallizing around two major, yet fundamentally different, strategic initiatives. These platforms, ai.gov and the National AI Research Resource (NAIRR) Pilot, represent contrasting philosophies on AI adoption, procurement, and governance. One embodies a top-down, centralized service and monitoring model aimed at efficiency and control, while the other promotes a bottom-up, federated research model focused on democratization and safety. Analysis of their public-facing technical artifacts provides a uniquely clear view into the architectural choices and strategic priorities shaping the future of AI in government.

### **Platform Analysis: The ai.gov Initiative (A Top-Down Service & Monitoring Model)**

The ai.gov project, inadvertently brought to public light through a temporarily accessible GitHub repository, offers an unprecedentedly transparent view into a state-level strategy for a "whole-of-government, AI-first" approach. Its development under the purview of the General Services Administration's (GSA) Technology Transformation Services (TTS) signals its intended role as a central procurement hub and service delivery vehicle for the entire federal apparatus. Although the original repository was quickly hidden after public discovery, it was subsequently located in an archived state, providing a primary source of exceptional value for OSINT analysis.

#### **Technical Breakdown**

A detailed examination of the archived gsa-tts-archived/ai.gov repository reveals a sophisticated, modern software project designed for rapid development and deployment of web-based services.

* **Repository & Technology Stack:** The project is structured as a monorepo managed with Turborepo and pnpm, a modern approach that optimizes for multi-package architectures. The technology stack is heavily weighted towards contemporary web development frameworks, with the primary languages being TypeScript (41.6%), the Astro web framework (27.0%), and the Svelte UI component framework (18.0%). This choice of technologies indicates a focus on building high-performance, component-based user interfaces. The repository is clearly divided into distinct applications, most notably apps/site, which contains the code for the public-facing portal, and apps/console, which houses the private administrative dashboard.  
* **The "All-in-One API":** This component is architected to function as a unified gateway, abstracting away the complexity for federal agencies to access a suite of large language models from leading commercial providers, including OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic. Leaked API documentation and code analysis confirm that the platform intends to leverage Amazon Bedrock as the underlying service provider. This choice aligns with a strategic focus on utilizing models that have achieved Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP) certification, a critical security requirement for government cloud services. However, the initial documentation also listed models from vendors like Cohere, which at the time were not confirmed to be FedRAMP certified, highlighting potential compliance and security gaps in the project's early stages.  
* **The "CONSOLE" Monitoring Tool:** Described in staging site materials as a "groundbreaking tool to analyze agency-wide implementation," the CONSOLE is a core feature of the ai.gov platform. Its explicit purpose is to provide senior government managers with a real-time dashboard to monitor AI usage across all federal agencies. This includes tracking which specific AI tools and models employees are using and which are most popular within their organizations. The existence and importance of this functionality are corroborated by the dedicated apps/console directory within the project's monorepo, which contains the source code for this private dashboard.

#### **Government Usage Patterns & Implications**

The stated purpose of ai.gov is to accelerate innovation, automate work, and streamline research and problem-solving for federal employees. However, its architecture reveals a more complex, dual purpose: it is designed not only for service delivery but also for the centralized surveillance and analysis of government employee activity. This structure has profound implications for the future of public administration.  
At a primary level, the platform is an exercise in standardization. It aims to simplify and rationalize the adoption of AI across the vast and often fragmented U.S. federal government. By creating a single, managed API gateway, the GSA can enforce procurement standards, such as the preference for FedRAMP-certified vendors, and exert control over costs and vendor relationships. This addresses a significant challenge in government IT, where individual agencies often procure redundant or incompatible systems.  
At a deeper level, the architecture establishes a powerful mechanism for internal surveillance and data collection. The CONSOLE feature, by design, will capture granular data on the AI tool usage of every federal employee who interacts with the platform. This creates a massive, centralized dataset detailing the decision-making processes, information-seeking behaviors, workflow patterns, and intellectual outputs of the entire federal bureaucracy.  
This leads to a critical, and likely unstated, long-term potential of the platform. The "data exhaust" generated by the widespread use of ai.gov is, itself, a prime candidate for a subsequent layer of artificial intelligence analysis. One can envision a meta-AI system being trained on the comprehensive data collected by the CONSOLE. Such a system could be tasked with identifying systemic inefficiencies, predicting policy implementation bottlenecks, modeling the flow of information across agencies, or even learning to automate complex administrative tasks at a macro scale.  
Therefore, the ai.gov architecture should be understood not merely as a service platform but as a foundational infrastructure for a recursively self-optimizing, and potentially self-automating, governmental administrative system. It represents a tangible step toward the automation of bureaucracy itself, a development with profound and largely undebated implications for public administration, the future of government labor, and the nature of democratic oversight.

This primary source material is cross-referenced and corroborated by investigative reports from news outlets such as The Register , ZDNet , and others , which captured details from the live staging site and initial repository before it was made private.

### **Platform Analysis: The National AI Research Resource (NAIRR) Pilot (A Federated Research Enablement Model)**

In stark contrast to the top-down, service-delivery model of ai.gov, the National AI Research Resource (NAIRR) Pilot represents a public-facing, congressionally-mandated initiative with a fundamentally different philosophy. Led by the National Science Foundation (NSF), its stated goal is not to provide AI as a service to government employees, but to democratize access to the fundamental building blocks of AI—compute, data, and tools—for the nation's broader research and education community. It embodies a decentralized, collaborative, and bottom-up approach to fostering a national AI ecosystem.

#### **Technical Breakdown**

The NAIRR is not a monolithic platform but is architected as a "shared national research infrastructure". Its technical capabilities are derived from a federation of contributed resources from a coalition of 12 federal agencies and 26 non-governmental partners. The pilot program is organized into four operational focus areas that define its architecture:

* **NAIRR Open:** Led by the NSF, this is the primary user-facing component, providing access to a diverse range of AI resources through the NAIRR Pilot Portal and a system of coordinated allocations.  
* **NAIRR Secure:** Co-led by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and the Department of Energy (DOE), this area is dedicated to supporting AI research that requires privacy- and security-preserving resources, assembling exemplar datasets and compute environments for sensitive work.  
* **NAIRR Software:** Also led by the NSF, this component focuses on facilitating and investigating the interoperable use of AI software, platforms, tools, and services across the various contributed resources.  
* **NAIRR Classroom:** This area is focused on outreach, aiming to reach new communities through education, training, and user support to broaden participation in AI research.

The "technical stack" of NAIRR is therefore not a centralized set of technologies but a distributed collection of high-value assets contributed by its partners. These include:

* **Compute Resources:** Access to significant high-performance computing (HPC) resources, including allocations on the DOE's Summit pre-exascale supercomputer and various NSF-supported AI testbeds.  
* **Data Resources:** A rich collection of AI-ready datasets from numerous federal agencies. Notable contributions include Earth science and foundational model data from NASA, biomedical datasets from the NIH, environmental data from NOAA, and geological data from the USGS.  
* **Tools and Models:** The pilot provides access to a suite of advanced software and models. This includes open-source tools from the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) designed for the assurance of autonomous systems and for testing robustness against adversarial attacks, a "responsible AI toolkit" from the Department of Defense (DoD), and a library of pre-trained models for various research applications.

#### **Government Usage Patterns & Implications**

The primary function of the NAIRR Pilot is to support fundamental, translational, and use-inspired AI research, with a stated emphasis on advancing "trustworthy AI" and tackling major societal challenges. Unlike the closed-access CONSOLE of ai.gov, access to NAIRR resources is managed through a public-facing portal (nairrpilot.org) and requires a formal application process, reinforcing its identity as a research-sponsoring entity.  
At a primary level, NAIRR's function is to level the playing field. It provides academic researchers, students, and institutions that lack the financial resources of large technology companies with access to the industrial-scale compute and proprietary data necessary to conduct cutting-edge AI research.  
This mission, however, reveals a fundamental strategic tension within the U.S. government's overall AI strategy. While the ai.gov initiative appears geared towards the procurement and *consumption* of existing, often closed-source commercial AI models, NAIRR is explicitly designed to *foster the creation* of new, open, academic, and trustworthy AI systems. This dichotomy highlights two parallel and potentially conflicting priorities: one arm of the government is focused on the rapid, centralized deployment of existing technology for immediate efficiency gains, while another is carefully nurturing a diverse, secure, and open domestic research ecosystem for long-term strategic advantage.  
This leads to a more nuanced understanding of NAIRR's strategic purpose. The platform's specific inclusion of resources from agencies like DARPA for "robustness against adversarial attacks" and from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) on "AI governance, risk management, and testing" is highly significant. The government is not merely funding the development of new AI models; it is actively trying to build a national community of researchers who are technically equipped to understand, analyze, and mitigate the myriad *risks* associated with AI. NAIRR is, in effect, an infrastructure for building a national-level "immune system" for AI safety and security.  
By providing the tools to attack and defend AI systems alongside the data and compute required to build them, the NAIRR Pilot is creating a national-scale, distributed "Red vs. Blue Team" environment for AI itself. The long-term strategic objective may therefore be less about competing directly with commercial model development and more about creating a generation of American researchers who are world-leading experts in AI safety, assurance, and trustworthiness. In an era of rapid and potentially destabilizing AI proliferation, cultivating this human expertise is a critical national security asset.

## **Autonomous Systems in Law Enforcement and Predictive Justice**

One of the most widespread and publicly scrutinized applications of autonomous decision-making in the public sector is predictive policing. These systems, which use historical data to forecast crime risk and direct police patrols, automate a core function of law enforcement and have become a focal point for debates on efficiency, equity, and algorithmic bias. The availability of rigorous academic research alongside public code repositories provides a uniquely transparent case study into the technical underpinnings of these systems and their documented societal impacts.

### **Algorithm Analysis: Predictive Policing Models**

An examination of public GitHub repositories and academic papers reveals the technical architecture of systems designed to predict crime. These systems primarily rely on two classes of algorithms, both of which are trained on historical data generated by police departments.

#### **Technical Breakdown**

* **Core Algorithmic Models:** The technical approaches to crime prediction can be broadly categorized into statistical time-series models and more conventional machine learning classifiers.  
  * **Self-Exciting Point Processes:** The presence of a Jupyter notebook titled hawkes.ipynb in the adewes/predpol repository strongly suggests the use of Hawkes processes. A Hawkes process is a type of self-exciting point process model, which is particularly well-suited for modeling phenomena where the occurrence of one event increases the probability of subsequent events in the near future—an "aftershock" effect. This aligns perfectly with criminological theories of retaliatory violence or "near repeat" burglaries, where one crime is a strong predictor of another nearby. This methodology is consistent with academic descriptions of the proprietary algorithm used by the company PredPol.  
  * **Classification and Regression Models:** A more common approach found in open-source projects involves framing crime prediction as a standard machine learning problem. Repositories demonstrate the use of various models, including Logistic Regression for binary risk classification , Random Forest classifiers for predicting crime types or high-risk areas , and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) for classifying incidents based on proximity to past events. For instance, the hannabodnar/predictive-policing repository, a project from Georgia Tech's OMSA program, uses a Random Forest model with geospatial features to predict the Los Angeles Police Department reporting district for a given crime, achieving a reported accuracy of 99.7%.  
* **Data Inputs and Inherent Bias:** A critical, unifying feature of all these systems is their exclusive reliance on historical police data as the ground truth for training. The datasets typically include features such as the type of crime, date, time, and geographic coordinates of past incidents. This reliance is the central point of failure from an equity perspective. It is well-documented that official police records are not an objective measure of all crime that occurs; rather, they are a record of where police have chosen to patrol and make arrests. As such, these datasets are heavily influenced by historical and ongoing human biases in policing, which can lead to the over-representation of crime in minority and low-income communities.

#### **Government Usage Patterns & Documented Impact**

The deployment of these algorithms is intended to make policing more efficient by directing scarce patrol resources to areas where they are most likely to be needed. However, rigorous academic studies have provided causal evidence of a significant and troubling trade-off.

* **The Causal Impact Study:** The research paper "Algorithmic Policing" by Ranae Jabri provides a landmark analysis of a PredPol system in a major urban jurisdiction. The study's strength lies in its quasi-experimental research design, which exploits an exogenous change in the set of crime types used by the PredPol algorithm to generate its predictive "boxes." This change allowed the researcher to isolate the *causal* effect of algorithm-induced police presence from other confounding factors.  
* **Key Findings on Effectiveness and Disparity:** The study produced two critical findings. First, it found that algorithm-induced police presence does, in fact, cause a statistically significant decrease in serious property and violent crime in the targeted areas. Second, and in stark contrast, it found that this same police presence simultaneously *increases* racial disparities in arrests. This effect was most pronounced for lower-level offenses, such as traffic incidents, where police officers exercise greater discretion. The presence of police in algorithm-designated hotspots led to disproportionately more arrests of Black individuals for these types of offenses, while arrests of white individuals for serious crimes actually decreased.  
* **The "Runaway Feedback Loop" Mechanism:** The mechanism that explains this documented trade-off is the "runaway feedback loop," a concept mathematically formalized in the arXiv paper 1706.09847. The process works as follows:  
  1. The algorithm, trained on biased historical data, predicts a "hotspot" in a neighborhood that has been historically over-policed.  
  2. Police are dispatched to this designated area.  
  3. The increased police presence naturally leads to more observed incidents and arrests in that area, particularly for discretionary offenses.  
  4. This newly generated arrest data is then fed back into the algorithm as training data for the next cycle.  
  5. The algorithm interprets this new data as "proof" that its original prediction was correct, reinforcing the area's status as a hotspot. This creates a self-perpetuating cycle where police are "repeatedly sent back to the same neighborhoods regardless of the true underlying crime rate," effectively automating and amplifying the initial bias in the data.

#### **Synthesis and Implications**

The case of predictive policing offers a powerful lesson in the real-world application of autonomous systems in government. While the primary insight is that these algorithms automate the allocation of police resources, a deeper analysis reveals a more complex and problematic dynamic.  
The causal relationship is clear: the use of biased historical data as the sole input directly causes the algorithms to produce biased outputs. When police act on these outputs, their actions generate more of the same biased data, which is then used to validate and retrain the system. This creates a closed, self-perpetuating system that is structurally incapable of correcting its own biases.  
This system effectively functions as a "bias laundering" mechanism. It takes subjective, discretionary, and historically biased human policing decisions—such as where to patrol, who to stop, and what to record—and transforms them into a seemingly objective, quantitative, and scientific directive: a red box on a digital map. This process serves to mask and legitimize existing institutional biases under a veneer of data-driven objectivity. It makes discriminatory patterns of policing harder to identify and challenge, because the source of the decision is no longer simply an officer's individual discretion but a complex, often proprietary "black-box" algorithm.  
Therefore, the deployment of these systems goes beyond simply automating policing; it is automating and entrenching historical injustice, making it more efficient, more scalable, and significantly less accountable to public oversight.

## **Autonomous Systems in National Cybersecurity**

In the critical state security domain of cybersecurity, the MITRE Caldera platform stands out as the foremost public example of an autonomous decision-making system. Developed by MITRE, a federally funded research and development center (FFRDC), and actively used and extended in partnership with government agencies like the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), Caldera provides a deeply documented and architecturally transparent case study of how autonomous capabilities are being operationalized for national security purposes.

### **Platform Analysis: MITRE Caldera**

Caldera is an open-source framework designed to automate adversary emulation, assist in manual red-teaming, and automate incident response. Its entire architecture is built upon the MITRE ATT\&CK® framework, a globally recognized knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques. This foundation provides a standardized, structured language for describing and executing cyber operations, which is essential for its autonomous capabilities.

#### **Technical Breakdown: The Decision-Making Architecture**

The heart of Caldera's autonomy is not a single algorithm but a sophisticated, modular architecture designed for adaptive, goal-oriented planning and execution.

* **Core System and Plugins:** The framework consists of a core system that provides an asynchronous command-and-control (C2) server, a REST API for programmatic interaction, and a web-based user interface. The system's power and flexibility come from its extensive use of plugins, which are separate repositories that add new agents, adversary profiles, and, most importantly, new decision-making logic.  
* **Planners and "Buckets": The Logic of Autonomy:** Caldera's autonomous decision-making is encapsulated within modules called **Planners**. A planner is a Python module that contains the logic for how a running operation should make decisions about which abilities to use and in what order. The fundamental construct that enables this decision-making is the **"bucket"**. Buckets represent the discrete states in a planner's state machine. By design, these buckets are intended to correspond directly to the high-level tactics in the MITRE ATT\&CK framework, such as 'Privilege Escalation', 'Discovery', 'Lateral Movement', and 'Collection'. The autonomous operation proceeds as the planner transitions between these buckets. Within each bucket, the planner's logic selects specific abilities (implementations of ATT\&CK techniques) to execute. The selection is not random; it is based on the "facts" that the Caldera agent has learned from the target environment. For example, if an agent discovers a fact indicating an open SSH port on a neighboring machine (host.service.ssh), the planner can use this fact to decide to execute an SSH brute-force ability. This ability to react and adapt its plan based on real-time information gathered from the environment is the hallmark of a true autonomous system, distinguishing it from a simple pre-scripted automation tool.  
* **Key Plugins for Autonomous Operations:**  
  * **Stockpile :** This is the foundational plugin that provides the default knowledge base for Caldera. It contains a large repository of abilities (implementations of TTPs) and pre-built adversary profiles. Planners draw from Stockpile to select their actions, making it the system's "arsenal."  
  * **Response :** This is arguably the most significant plugin from a strategic perspective. It is explicitly designed to enable **autonomous incident response**. The plugin contains abilities and adversary profiles for "Blue" (defensive) teams. By setting a single configuration flag, auto\_operation\_enable, to True, a user can instruct Caldera to automatically launch a Blue response operation immediately upon the detection of a Red (offensive) operation. This feature represents a concrete step toward fully automated cyber conflict.  
  * **BountyHunter :** This plugin introduces a more advanced, goal-oriented planner. Instead of deterministically following a state machine, the Bounty Hunter planner uses a weighted-random, reward-driven logic to select its next ability. This adds a degree of unpredictability, making the emulated attacks more realistic and harder to defend against. Crucially, it explicitly adds support for fully autonomous initial access and privilege escalation, enabling the emulation of complete, end-to-end attack chains.  
  * **Caldera for OT :** This is a major extension to the Caldera ecosystem, developed in direct partnership with CISA. It consists of a suite of plugins that add abilities for common industrial control system (ICS) and operational technology (OT) protocols, such as BACnet, DNP3, Modbus, and IEC 61850\. This extension directly applies Caldera's autonomous adversary emulation capabilities to the critical domain of national infrastructure protection.

#### **Government Usage Patterns & Implications**

Caldera is officially used for a range of government and enterprise security functions, including the automated testing of cyber defenses, training for blue teams, and facilitating red vs. blue team research exercises. CISA, in particular, uses the Caldera for OT extension to help its government and industry partners assess and improve the security of their OT systems.  
The primary insight from Caldera's architecture is that it successfully automates the complex process of emulating cyber attacks to validate defenses. However, a deeper analysis of its decision-making logic and plugin ecosystem reveals more profound strategic implications. The planner/bucket architecture enables the system to autonomously adapt its behavior in response to facts gathered from the target environment. This is not merely pre-scripted automation; it is genuine autonomous decision-making. The system observes, orients, decides, and acts in a continuous loop.  
This leads to a critical conclusion about the platform's ultimate potential. The existence of the Response plugin, which enables autonomous Blue team operations to counter Red team operations within the same integrated framework, is highly significant. Caldera is therefore not just an attack emulation tool; it is a complete, self-contained environment for modeling, simulating, and executing **autonomous machine-versus-machine conflict**. One instance of Caldera, configured with an offensive planner like BountyHunter, can be pitted directly against another instance configured with the defensive Response plugin. Both systems would then make autonomous decisions and counter-decisions based on the evolving state of the shared network environment, all without direct human intervention.  
The development and open-sourcing of this platform provide the technical foundation for the research, development, and testing of AI-driven cyber warfare agents. It represents a significant step in the operationalization of autonomous cyber capabilities, moving the paradigm from human-in-the-loop to human-on-the-loop, and pointing toward a future of fully autonomous engagements where the speed of conflict far exceeds the limits of human cognition and control.

## **Emerging Applications: Reinforcement Learning for Resource Optimization & Public Services**

Beyond the high-profile domains of law enforcement and cybersecurity, a growing body of research and open-source development is focused on applying autonomous decision-making techniques to complex, dynamic problems in public service delivery. Governments are constantly faced with large-scale resource allocation challenges under conditions of uncertainty, from managing hospital capacity during a pandemic to optimizing logistics for disaster relief. Reinforcement Learning (RL) and other optimization models are particularly well-suited for these types of sequential decision problems, and their application in the public sector is a significant emerging trend.

### **System Type: Dynamic Resource Allocation**

The challenge of allocating limited resources to meet fluctuating demand is a classic optimization problem. The advent of powerful computational techniques has enabled the development of sophisticated decision-support systems that can autonomously recommend or implement allocation strategies in real-time.

#### **Technical Breakdown**

* **Optimization Models:** A concrete, publicly available example of this approach is found in the sassoftware/medical-resource-optimization GitHub repository. This project provides a complete framework, written in SAS, for generating an optimal hospital restart plan, a problem of acute relevance during the COVID-19 pandemic. The system uses optimization algorithms—likely linear or mixed-integer programming, given the nature of the problem—to balance a set of competing objectives, including hospital economics (revenue, margin), the efficient use of constrained resources (ICU beds, ventilators, COVID-19 test kits), and the quality of clinical care (patient access, meeting demand). The model is highly parameterized, taking numerous data tables as input, such as resource capacity, historical utilization, financial data, and demand forecasts. A key feature is its ability to run multiple scenarios with different parameter settings, allowing decision-makers to explore the trade-offs between different policy choices (e.g., prioritizing revenue versus maximizing patient throughput).  
* **Reinforcement Learning Models:** For problems that are more dynamic and where the optimal policy is not known in advance, Reinforcement Learning offers a more powerful approach. Academic research provides clear demonstrations of this potential. One study published in a clinical therapy journal used deep reinforcement learning to train an agent to find optimal public health strategies for controlling the spread of COVID-19. In this model, the RL agent's "policy" represents a strategy for implementing interventions over time (e.g., adjusting lockdown intensity). The agent learns this policy through trial-and-error in a simulated environment, receiving "rewards" or "penalties" based on outcomes like infection rates and economic impact. The goal is to discover a sequence of actions that maximizes the cumulative reward over the long term. This RL-based approach is explicitly proposed in the public health literature as an ideal model for a range of complex decision problems, including resource allocation, adaptive monitoring strategies, and targeted sampling for hidden populations.

#### **Government Usage Patterns & Implications**

While many of these systems are currently in the research and planning stages, they indicate a clear trajectory toward the use of autonomous decision-support systems in government crisis management and public service administration. The profound policy implications of deploying such systems are already being considered. The whitepaper "Choices, Risks, and Reward Reports: Charting Public Policy for Reinforcement Learning Systems" directly confronts the governance challenges of using RL in critical public domains like energy infrastructure management and transportation systems. The authors argue that because these systems make autonomous decisions that have real-world consequences, traditional oversight mechanisms are insufficient. They propose the creation of a "Reward Report," a new form of documentation analogous to a "model card" or "datasheet," which would be required for any RL system deployed in the public sector.  
This proposal highlights a crucial aspect of these systems: the reward function as a direct encoding of public policy. In any reinforcement learning system, the *reward function* is the mathematical expression of the system's goals. It is a precise, coded definition of what constitutes a "good" or "bad" outcome. The decision of what to reward—for example, in the hospital optimization model, whether to prioritize maximizing revenue or minimizing patient wait times—is a fundamental policy decision with significant ethical and political dimensions. Yet, this critical choice is often buried deep within the system's code, opaque to public scrutiny and debate. The design of the reward function becomes a new and powerful site of policy-making.  
The "Reward Report" concept is a direct response to this governance gap. By requiring a transparent, public document that clearly articulates the design choices, trade-offs, and ethical considerations embedded within an RL system's reward function, policymakers and the public can begin to exercise meaningful oversight over these powerful new forms of automated governance. Without such mechanisms, there is a significant risk that critical public policy decisions will be delegated to unaccountable algorithmic systems.

## **Synthesis, Strategic Implications, and Recommendations**

The investigation into publicly available artifacts of autonomous decision-making systems reveals a rapidly evolving and strategically significant landscape within government operations. The transition from isolated experiments to integrated platforms is well underway, driven by distinct architectural patterns and a common set of underlying technologies. However, this technological progress is shadowed by profound governance challenges, as the automation of decision-making in domains like law enforcement, cybersecurity, and public services introduces new vectors for bias, risk, and unaccountable power.

### **Synthesis of Architectural Patterns**

Across the diverse applications examined, several recurring architectural and technological patterns emerge, pointing to a convergence in how state-level autonomous systems are being designed and deployed.

* **The Plugin-Based Ecosystem:** The architecture of MITRE Caldera exemplifies a powerful and increasingly common pattern: a modular core decision-making engine that can be extended with domain-specific capabilities via plugins. This approach allows for both standardization at the core and rapid specialization for new use cases. The Caldera for OT extension, which adds capabilities for industrial control systems, is a prime example of this model's success. This pattern of a stable core with a dynamic ecosystem of extensions is likely to be replicated in other large-scale government AI platforms as it provides a balance between centralized control and decentralized innovation.  
* **The Centrality of Reinforcement Learning:** Reinforcement Learning (RL) is the persistent technical thread that connects the most advanced autonomous systems across different domains. It is the core logic behind the adaptive planning of the BountyHunter plugin in Caldera for cybersecurity , the proposed method for optimizing resource allocation in public services , and the academic models for shaping public health policy during pandemics. RL has become the de facto standard for any problem that requires an agent to learn an optimal strategy through dynamic interaction with an uncertain environment. Its prevalence signifies a move beyond simple predictive analytics toward systems that can learn and execute complex, multi-step policies.  
* **The Governance Dichotomy:** The stark contrast between the ai.gov initiative and the NAIRR Pilot reveals a fundamental strategic choice that governments face in the age of AI. The ai.gov model represents a top-down, centralized approach focused on control, efficiency, and the consumption of commercial AI services, with an embedded capacity for internal surveillance. The NAIRR model, conversely, represents a bottom-up, federated approach focused on research, democratization of access, and the cultivation of a national ecosystem for trustworthy and open AI development. These two models are not just different technical architectures; they are competing visions for the role of AI in a democratic state.

### **Functional Insights & Future Trajectories**

The analysis of these systems in their operational context points to several critical future trajectories with significant societal implications.

* **The Automation of Bias:** The deep dive into predictive policing provides empirical and theoretical proof that AI systems can function as "bias laundromats." By training on biased historical data, these systems automate, scale, and legitimize existing patterns of discrimination under a veneer of scientific objectivity. This represents a critical systemic risk for any government AI system that relies on historical data generated by human processes, from hiring and benefits allocation to criminal justice.  
* **The Automation of Conflict:** The MITRE Caldera platform, particularly with its Response plugin, points directly toward a future of autonomous, machine-speed cyber warfare. The platform provides the technical means to pit autonomous offensive agents against autonomous defensive agents, creating a cycle of action and reaction that operates far beyond the speed of human intervention. This development has profound implications for military doctrine, deterrence theory, and the international laws of armed conflict.  
* **The Automation of Bureaucracy:** The architecture of the ai.gov CONSOLE feature suggests a trajectory that moves beyond automating individual tasks to monitoring and eventually automating administrative and bureaucratic processes themselves. The data collected by such a system could be used to train a meta-AI to manage the very machinery of government. This potential for the automation of bureaucracy is a development with far-reaching and largely unexamined consequences for the nature of governance, public employment, and democratic accountability.

### **Recommendations for Policy and Oversight**

Based on the technical evidence and strategic implications identified in this report, the following recommendations are proposed to guide the responsible development and deployment of autonomous systems in the public sector:

1. **Mandate "Reward Reports" for Reinforcement Learning Systems:** For any RL-based system intended for deployment in a public sector context, government agencies should adopt a framework similar to that proposed in the "Choices, Risks, and Reward Reports" paper. This would require the creation and public release of a "Reward Report" that transparently documents the design choices, ethical trade-offs, and policy objectives that have been mathematically encoded into the system's reward function. This is a necessary first step toward ensuring public accountability for algorithmic governance.  
2. **Invest in "AI Red Teaming" as a Core Governance Function:** The federal government should significantly expand its investment in initiatives like the security-focused components of NAIRR and platforms like MITRE Caldera. Building a robust national capability for the independent testing, evaluation, and adversarial "red teaming" of government AI systems is critical. This capability must be used to proactively identify and mitigate risks, from external security vulnerabilities to internal failures like bias amplification, before systems are deployed.  
3. **Develop Technically-Grounded Procurement and Deployment Standards:** Government procurement guidance and AI policy frameworks must evolve beyond high-level principles to include concrete technical requirements. Before any autonomous decision-making system is deployed in a high-stakes, public-facing role, it should be required to meet standards for:  
   * **Interpretability:** The ability to explain *why* a particular decision was made.  
   * **Contestability and "Circuit-Breakers":** Clear mechanisms for human oversight and intervention, including the ability to immediately halt or override the system's operation.  
   * **Mandatory Pre-Deployment Testing:** Rigorous testing in simulated environments to specifically assess the system's propensity for creating feedback loops and amplifying bias.

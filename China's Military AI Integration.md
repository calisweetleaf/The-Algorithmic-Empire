# **The Dragon's Brain: A Comprehensive History of AI Integration in China's Military and State Apparatus (1980-2025)**

## **Executive Summary**

This report provides a comprehensive historical analysis of the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) within the military, defense agencies, and state structures of the People's Republic of China (PRC). Spanning over four decades from the 1980s to the present, this documented timeline and assessment reveals that China's current push for "intelligentized warfare" is not a recent phenomenon spurred by the global deep learning boom, but the culmination of a deliberate, patient, and state-directed strategic vision.  
The narrative begins in the 1980s, a period marked by China's initial, cautious steps into AI research, formalized under the national **"863 Program."** This foundational era saw the People's Liberation Army (PLA) develop an early interest in "expert systems" for wargaming and decision support, establishing a long-term ambition to automate command and control that persists to this day. The "informatization shock" of the 1991 Gulf War served as a critical catalyst, forcing the PLA to recognize its profound technological deficit and embark on a two-decade-long mission to build the digital infrastructure—the command systems, networks, and data architecture—that would become the essential prerequisite for its future AI aspirations.  
The contemporary era, beginning in the 2010s and personally championed by General Secretary Xi Jinping, marks a strategic inflection point. The national goal shifted from "informatization" (catching up) to **"intelligentization" (智能化)**—a bid to "leapfrog" competitors and define the next paradigm of warfare. This ambition was codified in the 2017 **New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan**, a whole-of-nation blueprint that explicitly links civilian AI progress to military modernization. The engine driving this transformation is the **Military-Civil Fusion (军民融合)** strategy, a sophisticated ecosystem designed to systematically funnel resources from private industry, academia, and foreign technology acquisition directly into the PLA's warfighting capabilities.  
This report catalogs the tangible results of this strategy: the institutional embedding of AI research within premier military organizations like the **National University of Defense Technology (NUDT)** and the **Academy of Military Science (AMS)**; the development of specific AI-enabled platforms by state-owned defense giants like **China Electronics Technology Group Corporation (CETC)**; and the operational deployment of unmanned systems, intelligent command and control platforms, and autonomous algorithms across all PLA service branches. Analysis of PLA patents and research reveals a clear trajectory toward autonomous decision-making in combat, while the adaptation of open-source Western AI models like Meta's Llama for military intelligence highlights a strategy to bypass hardware restrictions and accelerate software development.  
Externally, Beijing projects an image of a responsible stakeholder, proposing AI governance principles at the United Nations that emphasize human control and ethical use. Internally, however, PLA doctrine, research, and development proceed with an unconstrained focus on achieving "decision dominance" and leveraging AI for a decisive military advantage. This dual-track approach—shaping international norms to potentially constrain rivals while pursuing military superiority—is a central feature of China's strategy.  
The PLA's path is not without significant challenges, including talent competition, data integration, system reliability, and hardware dependencies. Nevertheless, its trajectory is clear and aggressive. The systematic integration of AI into the Chinese military-industrial complex is fundamentally altering the strategic landscape, aiming to erode traditional military advantages, compress crisis decision timelines, and expand the battlefield into the cognitive domain. Understanding the deep historical roots and the intricate state-driven machinery of this transformation is critical for navigating the emerging era of strategic competition.

## **Part I: The Foundation \- From Theory to "Informatization" (1980-2010)**

The People's Liberation Army's current, highly publicized drive towards artificial intelligence is not a sudden sprint but the final leg of a marathon that began over forty years ago. This foundational period traces the origins of China's military AI ambitions, demonstrating a remarkable consistency of strategic thought that has persisted through dramatic technological and geopolitical shifts. From the earliest theoretical explorations in the 1980s, heavily influenced by global trends and codified by state-directed research programs, to the galvanizing shock of the 1990s that forced the PLA to build a digital nervous system, these decades laid the essential groundwork for the "intelligentized" military of the 21st century. This history reveals that the PLA was not merely reacting to the AI revolution of the 2010s; it was patiently preparing for it.

### **Chapter 1: The Seeds of Artifice (1980s) \- Early Explorations and State-Directed Research**

#### **The Global Context and China's Initial Steps**

As the world entered the 1980s, the field of artificial intelligence was experiencing a commercial and military boom, particularly in the West, centered on the development of "expert systems"—rule-based programs designed to replicate the decision-making of human specialists. For the People's Republic of China, which was just beginning to emerge from a period of relative technological isolation under Deng Xiaoping's "Reform and Opening Up" policy, this global trend did not go unnoticed. Recognizing a significant technological gap, Beijing initiated a structured, top-down approach to catch up.  
AI research, which had nascent beginnings in the late 1970s, was formally institutionalized with the establishment of the **Chinese Association for Artificial Intelligence (中国人工智能学会, CAAI)** in September 1981\. This organization, authorized by the Ministry of Civil Affairs, provided a national focal point for a scattered community of researchers and signaled the state's official interest in the field. Early efforts were characterized by sending scholars overseas to study AI and providing government funds for domestic research projects, a model of learning from the outside while building capacity at home.

#### **The "863 Program" \- A National Mandate for High Technology**

The most pivotal development of this era was the launch of the **State High-Tech Research and Development Plan (国家高技术研究发展计划)**, universally known as the **"863 Program" (863计划)**. Established in March 1986, the program was a direct response by China's leadership, including Deng Xiaoping, to the perceived strategic challenge posed by advanced technology initiatives in the West, most notably the U.S. Strategic Defense Initiative. The 863 Program was an explicit state intervention designed to mobilize national resources to stimulate the development of advanced technologies and render China independent of foreign reliance.  
Crucially, the program was designed from its inception with a dual-use focus, aiming to develop technologies applicable to both the civilian economy and military modernization. Among the seven original key technological fields targeted for investment were **Automation** and **Information Technology**, the two areas that would form the bedrock of all future AI development. The 863 Program thus created the first formal, high-level state funding and guidance structure for what would become China's military AI research ecosystem.

#### **Early PLA Interest in "Expert Systems"**

The PLA's engagement with AI during this period was not merely observational or academic; it was practical and aspirational. Drawing from the global interest in expert systems, PLA researchers began their own early initiatives to apply this form of rule-based AI to military problems. As documented by later analyses, these efforts, dating to the late 1980s and early 1990s, focused on military operations research and wargaming.  
This early adoption reveals a foundational and remarkably persistent ambition within the PLA: the desire to automate and optimize command and decision-making processes. The "expert systems" of the 1980s, which functioned by encoding the knowledge of human commanders into a series of "if-then" rules, are the direct conceptual ancestors of the sophisticated "intelligent command" (智能指挥) and autonomous decision systems the PLA pursues today. This demonstrates that the PLA's quest for AI in command and control is not a novel strategy born of the deep learning era, but the modern realization of a strategic objective that has been consistently held for over four decades. The ambition was present long before the technology was mature enough to fully achieve it.

#### **Key Institutions Take Root**

This period also saw the establishment and reform of the key academic and research institutions that would become the pillars of China's military AI development complex. The **National University of Defense Technology (国防科技大学, NUDT)**, often called the "Military's Tsinghua," was formally established in its modern form in 1978 and began to undertake advanced research projects indicative of complex control systems, such as its magnetic levitation transport research in the late 1980s. Concurrently, the **Academy of Military Science (军事科学院, AMS)**, the PLA's highest-level research institute, was tasked with studying foreign military developments and theorizing about the future of warfare, positioning it to become the PLA's primary doctrinal think tank for AI. These institutions, nurtured under state programs like 863, would form the intellectual core of the PLA's long-term technological strategy.

### **Chapter 2: The "Informatization" Shock (1990s-2000s) \- Building the Digital Battlefield**

#### **The Gulf War Catalyst**

If the 1980s planted the seeds of China's AI ambitions, the 1990s provided the harsh sunlight that forced them to grow. The 1991 Persian Gulf War was a profound and deeply unsettling event for the PLA's leadership. Watching the United States military conduct a swift and decisive campaign built on advanced network-centric warfare, precision-guided munitions, and integrated C4ISR (Command, Control, Communications, Computers, Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance) capabilities, Chinese military observers recognized the massive chasm between their own capabilities and those of a modern fighting force.  
The PLA at the time was still a force largely structured around the concept of "mechanized" (机械化) warfare, reliant on mass and industrial-age platforms. The Gulf War demonstrated that this paradigm was obsolete. This realization triggered an urgent, top-to-bottom reorientation of PLA modernization strategy. The new central goal, which would dominate the next two decades, was to achieve **"informatization" (信息化)**—the comprehensive integration of information technology across the entire military enterprise.

#### **Developing "Command Automation" and Laying the Foundation for Systems Confrontation**

The PLA understood that it could not leap directly to the cutting edge. It first had to build the digital foundation. This led to a concerted effort to develop what the PLA termed **"command automation" (指挥自动化)**, a concept comparable to Western C3 (Command, Control, and Communications) systems. While a pilot project for command automation had existed within the former Second Artillery Force (now the PLA Rocket Force) since 1973, it was in the 1990s and 2000s that these initiatives gained real traction and began to be rolled out across the services.  
These early systems were often fragmented and faced significant hurdles due to China's relative technological backwardness at the time. However, their development was a critical step in building institutional knowledge and technical capacity. This process was guided by an evolving strategic doctrine. The PLA began to conceptualize future conflict not as a clash of individual tanks or ships, but as a **"systems confrontation" (体系对抗)**—a holistic struggle between two opposing operational systems, each comprising sensors, command nodes, and effectors linked by networks.  
This doctrine of systems warfare inherently requires a high degree of information fusion, network resiliency, and data processing capability. It reinforced the absolute necessity of achieving informatization. The PLA correctly identified that a truly intelligent, AI-driven military was impossible to build without first constructing a robust, secure, and integrated information architecture. The two-decade-long, single-minded focus on building networks, deploying sensors, and developing C4ISR platforms was not a detour from the path to AI; it was the deliberate and necessary paving of that path. They were building the digital highways before attempting to deploy the autonomous vehicles that would one day travel on them. As PLA doctrine would later explicitly state, "intelligentization" is built upon the prior stages of mechanization and informatization.

#### **Nascent Research in Unmanned Systems and Advanced AI**

While informatization was the main effort, parallel research into the technologies of the future continued. PLA strategists began to write about future "unmanned, intangible, silent warfare". The military started to acquire and experiment with unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs). A key early acquisition was the Israeli IAI Harpy anti-radiation drone in 1994, which the PLA later reverse-engineered. China's own defense industry began developing indigenous models like the Chang Hong-1 (CH-1) and various ASN-series reconnaissance drones, though these early platforms were limited in range and capability.  
In academia, research into more advanced AI concepts like neural networks continued, even as the field experienced a global "AI winter" due to unfulfilled promises. Chinese researchers at institutions like Tsinghua University published early papers on AI, and work on machine learning, including deep neural networks, laid the theoretical groundwork for the breakthroughs that would follow in the next decade. This quiet, persistent research ensured that when the global AI landscape shifted, China would be positioned to capitalize on it rapidly.

## **Part II: The Mandate \- "Intelligentization" as National Strategy (2011-Present)**

The period from 2011 onward marks a decisive inflection point in China's military modernization. Artificial intelligence transitioned from a background research topic and a component of "informatization" to the central, guiding principle of national security and military strategy. Under the personal and authoritative direction of General Secretary Xi Jinping, the concept of **"intelligentization" (智能化)** was elevated to a national mandate. This era is defined by top-level strategic planning, the systematic mobilization of national resources through the **Military-Civil Fusion (军民融合)** strategy, and the deliberate weaving of AI into the very fabric of the PLA's doctrine, organization, and institutional structure. It represents a fundamental shift in strategic thinking: from an effort to catch up with the West to a determined bid to "leapfrog" competitors and define the next era of warfare.

### **Chapter 3: The Rise of "Intelligentized Warfare" (智能化战争)**

#### **Xi Jinping's Authoritative Mandate**

Upon assuming leadership of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and the Central Military Commission (CMC), Xi Jinping made accelerating PLA modernization a top personal priority. He articulated a new vision for the military's technological evolution, moving beyond the established goal of informatization. The culminating moment came in his landmark report to the 19th Party Congress in October 2017, where he issued the authoritative directive for the PLA to **“Accelerate the development of military intelligentization (加快军事智能化发展)”**.  
This single phrase, delivered from the highest level of political power, officially elevated "intelligentization" to the guiding principle for the future of the Chinese military. It signaled a new stage in the PLA's development, building upon but ultimately superseding the previous eras of mechanization and informatization. This mandate was reinforced by increasingly urgent timelines. In 2020, the PLA set a new goal to "accelerate the integrated development of mechanization, informatization, and intelligentization" by the centennial of its founding in 2027, indicating a move to pursue all three modernization goals simultaneously rather than sequentially. At the 20th Party Congress in October 2022, Xi reiterated this urgency, calling on China to "speed up the development of unmanned, intelligent combat capabilities".

#### **The "New Generation AI Development Plan" (2017)**

To translate this high-level political vision into a concrete national strategy, the State Council issued the **New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan (新一代人工智能发展规划)** in July 2017\. This document is the foundational civil-military blueprint for China's AI ambitions. It laid out a three-step timeline with the ultimate goal of making China the world's premier AI innovation center by 2030, with the core AI industry exceeding 1 trillion RMB (approx. $140 billion) in value.  
Most critically for this analysis, the plan explicitly and repeatedly linked civilian AI development to national defense. It called for the deep implementation of the Military-Civil Fusion strategy to "promote the two-way conversion and application of military and civilian scientific and technological achievements" and form a "new pattern of in-depth military-civilian integration". The plan specifically mandated strengthening the use of AI in military applications, including:

* Intelligent command and control systems  
* Military deductions and wargaming  
* Development of intelligent defense equipment.

This plan codified the national policy that the fruits of China's burgeoning commercial AI sector would be systematically harvested for military power.

#### **Defining "Intelligentized Warfare"**

In parallel with these top-level directives, PLA strategists and theorists at institutions like the Academy of Military Science began to flesh out the doctrine of "intelligentized warfare" (智能化战争). They define it as a new, revolutionary form of warfare, evolving from and surpassing "informatized warfare," that is supported primarily by AI technologies and methods. Analysis of PLA writings reveals several core tenets of this new doctrine:

* **Human-Machine Collaborative Decision-Making:** The traditional command hierarchy is seen as too slow for the speed of modern conflict. The future command structure is envisioned as a "command brain" (指挥大脑) or an "extension of the human brain," where human commanders and AI systems are deeply integrated in a collaborative decision-making process. AI is expected to handle rapid data processing and tactical execution, while humans provide creativity and strategic oversight.  
* **Unmanned Systems as Central Combatants:** A fundamental shift is anticipated toward "machine-led warfare" (机器战争), where unmanned systems—drones, robotic vehicles, and autonomous naval vessels—become the primary executors of tactical actions on the front lines, with humans retreating to command and supervisory roles.  
* **Algorithmic Superiority:** Victory in intelligentized warfare is seen as being determined by a **"game of algorithms" (算法致胜)**. The side with the superior AI control algorithms, which can coordinate swarms of unmanned platforms more effectively and make faster, better decisions, will hold the decisive advantage. This is encapsulated in the concept of **"多胜精" (duō shèng jīng)**—the idea that a large number of intelligent, low-cost unmanned systems can achieve victory over a smaller number of exquisite, high-cost manned platforms.  
* **Cognitive Domain Operations:** The battlefield is seen as expanding beyond the physical domains into the cognitive space. "Cognitive confrontation" (认知对抗) aims to use AI-powered tools to analyze an adversary's cognitive weaknesses and deploy targeted information operations to degrade their decision-making capabilities and will to resist.

This doctrinal shift from "informatization" to "intelligentization" represents a profound change in China's strategic thinking. While informatization was primarily about closing a known technological gap with the U.S. military—emulating the American model of network-centric warfare—intelligentization is viewed as a historic opportunity to "leapfrog" competitors. PLA writings frame AI as a disruptive technology that will allow China to "design" future warfare, creating entirely new mechanisms for victory rather than simply competing on established terms. It is a move from a reactive, catch-up posture to a proactive, paradigm-setting one.

### **Chapter 4: Military-Civil Fusion (军民融合) as the Engine**

The grand strategy of intelligentization is powered by an equally ambitious implementation mechanism: **Military-Civil Fusion (军民融合, MCF)**. Personally overseen by Xi Jinping, who chairs the Central Commission for Military-Civil Fusion Development, MCF is the national strategy designed to eliminate the traditional barriers between China's civilian research and commercial sectors and its military and defense industrial sectors. Its explicit goal is to systematically reorganize the entire Chinese science and technology enterprise to ensure that new innovations simultaneously advance both economic and military development, fueling the PLA's transformation into a "world-class military" by 2049\.  
MCF is not a vague policy preference but a concrete, whole-of-nation system with several key mechanisms of action, particularly in the AI domain:

* **Dual-Use Technology Transfer:** The strategy actively promotes a two-way flow of technology and resources. It encourages state-owned defense enterprises to spin off technologies for commercial use ("military-to-civilian," 军转民). More critically, it creates pathways for "civilian-to-military" (民参军) transfer, allowing the PLA to tap into the innovation, capital, and talent of the more dynamic private sector.  
* **Co-opting Private Industry as a "National Team":** Under MCF, China's leading private technology companies are enlisted as "national champions" in the pursuit of strategic goals. The 2017 AI Development Plan formalized this by launching a series of national "open innovation platforms," each led by a tech giant. Baidu was tasked with autonomous driving, Alibaba with "city brain" (smart cities), Tencent with medical imaging, and iFlytek with voice intelligence. These are all dual-use technologies with direct applications in military intelligence, logistics, and autonomous systems.  
* **Joint Research and Development:** A core feature of MCF is the establishment of joint laboratories that pair military research institutes with civilian technology firms. The premier example is the **"Joint Laboratory for Intelligent Command and Control Technologies" (智能指挥控制技术联合实验室)**, unveiled in 2018\. This partnership brings together Baidu, China's leader in AI and cloud computing, with the 28th Research Institute of the state-owned defense conglomerate CETC, a key developer of PLA electronic warfare and command systems. This formal linkage creates a direct pipeline for transferring cutting-edge civilian AI, big data, and cloud computing expertise into the development of the PLA's next-generation command and control systems.  
* **Leveraging Academia:** Top civilian universities are integral to the MCF ecosystem. Tsinghua University, often called "China's MIT," has publicly affirmed its deep commitment to the strategy. Its Vice President, You Zheng, has highlighted the university's role in military AI research, including a major project on **"AI Theories and Crux Technologies for Future Human-Machine Cooperative (Combat) Operations" (面向未来人机协同作战的人工智能理论与关键技术)**, which received over 100 million RMB (approx. $15 million) in funding from the CMC Science and Technology Commission. This demonstrates a direct flow of military funding into a civilian university to solve specific military AI challenges.  
* **Acquisition of Foreign Technology:** The MCF strategy is not limited to domestic resources. It explicitly includes the acquisition of the world's cutting-edge technologies through a variety of means—including licit channels like academic collaborations and overseas investments, as well as illicit methods such as industrial espionage, cyber theft, and forced technology transfer—all with the goal of diverting these innovations to advance the PLA's military capabilities.

This intricate network of policies and institutions reveals that MCF is not a loose collection of initiatives but a highly structured ecosystem. It is designed to function as a deliberate "innovation pipeline," systematically channeling the full spectrum of national resources—from state-funded academic research and private-sector agility to acquired foreign technology—directly into the PLA's military modernization programs. This process can be traced from end to end: a national directive like the 2017 AI plan provides the top-level guidance; a military body like the CMC funds a specific research project at a top university like Tsinghua; a private tech leader like Baidu collaborates with a state-owned defense firm like CETC on development; and CETC ultimately produces and fields an operational military system for the PLA. This is the Military-Civil Fusion pipeline in action, a state-driven model designed to rival and, in Beijing's view, surpass the more diffuse defense innovation ecosystems of the West.

### **Chapter 5: Institutional Weaving \- Embedding AI Across the PLA**

The national strategies of intelligentization and Military-Civil Fusion are not merely abstract concepts; they are being actively implemented through a network of specialized institutions and woven into the organizational structure of the People's Liberation Army. This institutional embedding ensures that AI development is not siloed but is instead integrated directly with doctrinal development, operational requirements, and the specific missions of each service branch.

#### **The Central Role of Key Institutions**

Three types of organizations form the core of the PLA's AI development ecosystem: premier military universities, top-level military think tanks, and massive state-owned defense conglomerates.

* **National University of Defense Technology (NUDT):** As the PLA's most prestigious institution for science and engineering, NUDT is a primary engine of advanced military R\&D. Its contributions to AI are multifaceted. It developed the **Tianhe-series supercomputers**, which provide the massive computational power essential for training large AI models. More directly, NUDT's researchers are at the forefront of developing military-specific AI applications. This is evidenced by patents filed by the university, such as **CN114706418A**, which details a sophisticated method for **"unmanned aerial vehicle combat autonomous decision-making"** using advanced reinforcement learning algorithms to train fighter drones for air-to-air combat. NUDT is also a center for doctrinal innovation, with its researchers publishing concepts for using AI to conduct "precision strikes" in the cognitive domain.  
* **Academy of Military Science (AMS):** Historically the PLA's top institution for strategy and doctrine, the AMS was reformed under Xi Jinping to become the leading edge of the PLA's entire military scientific enterprise. Its mandate is to fuse cutting-edge technological innovation with new operational concepts. The AMS has established new research institutes focused on unmanned systems, AI, and other frontier technologies. Its researchers are actively engaging with the latest global AI trends, publishing studies on the military applications of generative AI and, in one notable case, detailing their use of Meta's open-source **Llama 13B model** to develop a specialized LLM for military open-source intelligence (OSINT) analysis. This demonstrates a hands-on approach to adapting available technology for military purposes.  
* **China Electronics Technology Group Corporation (CETC):** CETC is a sprawling state-owned defense conglomerate that serves as the primary systems integrator for the PLA's electronic and information warfare capabilities. It is a critical node in the MCF ecosystem, translating research into fielded systems. CETC is explicitly active in developing a wide range of military AI technologies, from unmanned systems and their countermeasures, like the **"SkyShield" (天盾)** anti-drone system, to the complex networks that form the PLA's command and control backbone, such as the **"Yuanmo" (远谋)** and **"Tianqiong" (天穹)** systems. Its formal collaboration with Baidu on intelligent command underscores its central role in operationalizing AI for the PLA.

#### **Integration into Service Branches**

AI is not being developed in a vacuum but is being tailored to the needs of the PLA's various service branches and new strategic forces.

* **PLA Strategic Support Force (PLASSF) (2015-2024):** Established in the 2015 military reforms, the PLASSF was a novel organization designed to integrate the PLA's strategic capabilities in the space, cyber, electronic, and psychological warfare domains. From its inception, it was given a mandate to innovate and was seen as being at the forefront of the PLA's push for "intelligent warfare". The SSF acted as a testbed for integrating emerging technologies and developing the concepts for information-centric conflict. Procurement data shows the SSF was a primary customer for AI-related systems from civilian research institutes. In April 2024, the PLASSF was dissolved and its functions were reorganized into three new, more specialized arms: the Aerospace Force, the Cyberspace Force, and the Information Support Force, a move likely intended to further streamline and enhance capabilities in these critical domains.  
* **PLA Rocket Force (PLARF):** As the custodian of China's conventional and nuclear missiles, the PLARF has the PLA's longest institutional history with command automation, dating back to a pilot project in 1973\. Today, its modernization focuses on intelligent systems to enhance its core mission. PLARF brigades are developing and deploying **"intelligent training and management systems"** that automatically collect and analyze thousands of data points from missile operations training, replacing manual processes and likely improving readiness and performance assessment. The PLARF is a key user of the PLA's **integrated command platform (一体化指挥平台)** and is seeking to leverage AI and big data to improve decision support, early warning, and targeting for both its conventional and nuclear forces.  
* **PLA Navy (PLAN) and PLA Air Force (PLAAF):** Both services are at the leading edge of integrating AI-enabled unmanned systems into their operational forces. The PLAN regularly deploys a variety of UAVs, such as the **BZK-005** and **TB-001**, for long-range maritime surveillance and reconnaissance. Critically, these drones are not operating in isolation; they are being integrated into mixed formations with crewed aircraft (like the KQ-200 anti-submarine warfare plane) and naval vessels in exercises around Taiwan and the South China Sea, suggesting a developing concept for manned-unmanned teaming. The PLAAF, meanwhile, is actively conducting "intelligentized warfare" exercises. These drills test advanced concepts and systems, such as Norinco's **"Intelligent Precision Strike System,"** which automates the entire kill chain from drone dispatch and target tracking to strike allocation, demonstrating a clear ambition to automate combat functions at machine speed.

## **Part III: The Arsenal \- Systems, Platforms, and Capabilities**

Moving from high-level strategy and institutional frameworks to the tangible outputs of China's four-decade-long pursuit of military AI, this section provides a detailed survey of the specific systems, platforms, and capabilities being developed and deployed. This "order of battle" for intelligentization reveals a military that is rapidly fielding a diverse and increasingly sophisticated arsenal of AI-enabled tools. These range from swarms of autonomous drones designed to overwhelm adversaries, to complex "command brain" networks intended to achieve decision dominance, to the specific algorithms that power autonomous combat and cognitive warfare operations.

### **Chapter 6: The Unmanned Vanguard (无人作战系统)**

Unmanned systems are the most visible manifestation of the PLA's intelligentization strategy. They are seen not merely as support assets but as central combatants in future warfare, designed to change the fundamental calculus of conflict.

#### **Doctrine and Concepts**

The PLA's doctrine for unmanned warfare is built on the concept of leveraging quantity and algorithmic coordination to defeat technologically superior but numerically inferior forces. PLA academic writings from sources like the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences explicitly discuss the shift from a traditional "heavy elite soldiers, light quantity and scale" (重精兵作战轻数量规模) approach to a new paradigm where **"small and many wins against large and few" (小而多”胜“大而少)**.  
This is the core idea behind **"swarm tactics" (蜂群战术)**. The PLA envisions deploying large numbers of low-cost, networked unmanned platforms to conduct multi-directional, saturating attacks that overwhelm an adversary's defensive systems, such as a carrier strike group's air defenses. By making platforms cheap and expendable, this approach aims to impose an unsustainable economic cost on an adversary, trapping them in a "can't afford to shoot, can't afford not to defend" (打不起、防不着) dilemma. This doctrine seeks to turn an adversary's reliance on expensive, exquisite platforms into a critical vulnerability.

#### **Platform Development**

China's defense industry has produced a vast and growing portfolio of unmanned systems for every domain.

* **Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs):** The PLA has moved far beyond its early reverse-engineered drones. It now operates a wide spectrum of platforms, from tactical reconnaissance models to large, long-endurance strategic systems. Key models in service include the **BZK-005**, **BZK-007**, and **TB-001** for maritime reconnaissance and surveillance; the widely exported **GJ-1/Wing Loong** series for integrated reconnaissance and strike missions; and the high-altitude, long-endurance (HALE) **WZ-7 "Soaring Dragon" (翔龙)**. More recent additions include the **ASN-301**, an anti-radiation "suicide drone" designed for suppressing enemy air defenses, and the stealthy **GJ-11 "Sharp Sword" (利剑)** unmanned combat aerial vehicle (UCAV).  
* **Unmanned Ground, Surface, and Underwater Vehicles (UGV/USV/UUV):** The PLA is rapidly expanding its unmanned capabilities on land and at sea. At the 2024 Zhuhai Airshow, China's defense industry unveiled the **VU-T10**, a medium tracked UGV designed as a flagship ground combat system. It features a modular design that can be equipped with a 30mm cannon, machine guns, and anti-tank missiles to perform reconnaissance and fire support missions. In the maritime domain, CETC has been deploying a network of fixed and floating unmanned platforms as part of its **"Blue Ocean Information Network" (蓝海信息网络)** in the South China Sea. These platforms are equipped with radars and other sensors to provide persistent surveillance, demonstrating an effort to build an unmanned sensor grid in contested waters. The PLA is also actively exploring the use of combat robot dogs for urban warfare, as demonstrated in joint exercises with Cambodia.

#### **Swarming and Manned-Unmanned Teaming (MUM-T)**

The PLA is moving beyond operating individual drones to coordinating them into cohesive fighting units. In June 2017, CETC successfully demonstrated the launch of a large fixed-wing drone swarm, showcasing its technical capability in this area. The military is actively pursuing Manned-Unmanned Teaming (MUM-T) concepts. This includes the development of "loyal wingman" type aircraft, like the **FH-97A**, which are designed to fly alongside and support crewed fighters. Operationally, the PLA is already practicing these concepts by integrating its BZK-005 UAVs with crewed anti-submarine warfare aircraft and naval destroyers in exercises. An article in the official *PLA Daily* describes how intelligent command systems will be used to control "aerial 'swarms,' underwater 'fish schools,' and land-based 'wolf packs'" of unmanned clusters in coordination with manned forces.

#### **Counter-Unmanned Aerial Systems (C-UAS)**

Recognizing that it will also be a target of unmanned systems, the PLA is investing heavily in a layered C-UAS defense.

* **CETC "SkyShield" (天盾) and "Tianqiong" (天穹):** These names refer to integrated, modular air defense systems developed by CETC, China's premier military electronics firm. These systems are designed to create a protective dome over critical assets. They combine multiple sensor types, including radar and electro-optical systems, for detection and tracking. For neutralization, they employ a mix of non-kinetic effectors, like electronic jammers to sever control links, and kinetic effectors, including a high-energy fiber laser weapon. The laser component is reportedly capable of delivering up to 30 kilowatts of power, allowing it to ablate and destroy a UAV's critical structures in seconds at a range of up to 4 kilometers.  
* **Layered Defense Architecture:** The PLA's broader C-UAS strategy emphasizes a multi-layered approach that combines different technologies. This includes portable phased-array radars specifically designed for drone detection, like the **YLC-48 "UAV Terminator,"** and passive radar systems like the **DWL002**, which can detect stealthy UAVs without emitting a signal. These detection systems are networked with a range of effectors, including high-power microwave (HPM) weapons like Norinco's **"Hurricane"** series, which are designed to fry the electronics of entire drone swarms simultaneously.

### **Chapter 7: The Intelligent Command Nucleus (智能指挥)**

At the heart of the PLA's intelligentization strategy is the transformation of its command and control (C2) apparatus. The goal has evolved dramatically from the simple "command automation" of the 1990s to the far more ambitious concept of **"intelligent command" (智能指挥)**. This new paradigm seeks to create a military **"command brain" (指挥大脑)**—a networked, AI-powered system of systems that can fuse massive amounts of battlefield data, predict enemy actions, generate optimal courses of action, and assist or even automate decision-making at a speed and scale that is impossible for human commanders to achieve alone.

#### **Key System Developments**

While much of this development is classified, open-source intelligence reveals the emergence of specific systems and concepts that form the core of this intelligent command nucleus.

* **CETC "Yuanmo" (远谋) Integrated C2 System:** The name "Yuanmo" translates to "far-reaching strategy," indicating its high-level purpose. This integrated command and control system, developed by the state-owned giant CETC, appears to be a central component of the PLA's new C2 architecture. It is designed to be networked with sensor systems like the **"Tianqiong"** air defense network. The Yuanmo system's function is to synchronize and fuse data from disparate sources—radar, electro-optical sensors, electronic warfare intercepts—and use AI algorithms to automate threat assessments and coordinate rapid, multi-domain countermeasures.  
* **Norinco "Intelligent Precision Strike System":** This system, developed by another major state-owned defense contractor, Norinco, provides a clear example of an automated kill chain in practice. As demonstrated in a recent PLA exercise, the system performs a sequence of tasks that were once the domain of human analysts and commanders. It autonomously dispatches reconnaissance drones, uses their real-time video and sensor feeds to model the battlefield, identifies and tracks high-value targets, devises an optimal strike plan, and then automatically distributes targeting data to the most appropriate weapon systems (such as artillery or other drones) for execution. This represents a significant step towards a fully automated reconnaissance-strike loop.  
* **Large Language Models (LLMs) in Command:** PLA researchers are moving quickly to incorporate the power of generative AI and large language models into command and control. A 2025 article in *PLA Daily* explicitly discusses how military LLMs will become a mainstream direction for AI development, capable of replacing low-level planning and analysis tasks. Researchers at the PLA National Defense University have highlighted LLMs as pivotal for streamlining intelligence analysis and accelerating weapons development. A concrete example is the **"Aiwu LLM+" (爱武大模型+)** system, developed by a lab at the People's Armed Police Engineering University. It combines LLMs with multimodal data analysis and a virtual assistant interface to provide "intelligent interaction and task planning" within command information systems, effectively acting as an AI assistant for military commanders.

#### **The Goal: Decision Dominance**

The ultimate purpose of these intelligent command systems is to achieve what PLA theorists call "decision dominance" (决策优势). By leveraging AI to process information and execute commands faster than an adversary, the PLA aims to consistently and decisively outmaneuver its opponents. The goal is to dramatically accelerate the OODA (Observe-Orient-Decide-Act) loop, seizing the initiative and creating battlefield dynamics that an enemy relying on traditional, human-centric command processes cannot counter.

### **Chapter 8: The Algorithmic Battlefield \- From ISR to Cognitive Warfare**

Beyond physical platforms and command networks, the true core of intelligentized warfare lies in the software and algorithms that perceive the battlefield, make decisions, and shape the information environment. The PLA is pursuing a multi-pronged effort to develop and deploy these algorithms, moving from data analysis to autonomous action and into the new domain of cognitive conflict.

#### **Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (ISR)**

AI is the only viable solution for making sense of the deluge of data produced by the PLA's expanding sensor networks. The primary goal is to achieve a "transparent battlefield" by intelligently fusing data from satellites, drones, ships, and ground-based sensors. A key focus area is **Automated Target Recognition (ATR)**. Analysis of PLA procurement contracts reveals a concerted effort to acquire AI-based ATR software, with specific contracts issued for target detection algorithms for UAVs, feature extraction from synthetic aperture radar (SAR) imagery, and multi-target fusion systems. This automates the laborious process of identifying enemy assets, allowing for faster and more accurate situational awareness.

#### **Autonomous Decision-Making (自主决策系统)**

The PLA's ambition extends beyond decision *support* to true decision *autonomy*. The goal is to create systems that can independently analyze a situation and execute a mission without direct human control. Concrete evidence of this research and development can be found in the PLA's patent filings.

* **Patent CN114706418A**, filed by the **National University of Defense Technology (NUDT)**, describes a **"UAV combat autonomous decision-making method"**. This is not a theoretical paper but a detailed engineering blueprint for an autonomous air-to-air combat system. It uses a Markov Decision Process to model the combat environment and employs an advanced deep reinforcement learning algorithm, Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3), to train a neural network to generate optimal flight maneuvers in real-time. The system's goal is to achieve an advantageous position (e.g., behind the enemy) and a weapons lock, all without human intervention.  
* **Patent CN112307622B**, filed by the **Ordnance Science and Research Academy of China**, details a **"computer-generated forces autonomous planning system"**. This system is designed for ground units and uses a deep convolutional neural network to analyze battlefield data and assess threat levels. Based on this assessment, it uses a modified A\* search algorithm to autonomously plan the safest and most effective path for a unit to reach its objective.

These patents provide indisputable, technical proof that the PLA and its affiliated research bodies are actively engineering the core components of autonomous weapon systems, moving far beyond the "human-in-the-loop" concepts often discussed in public forums.

#### **Generative AI for Intelligence and Cognitive Warfare**

The recent explosion in generative AI has been rapidly integrated into the PLA's strategic thinking.

* **Intelligence Analysis and the "Open-Source Leapfrog":** The PLA and its defense industry are creating specialized generative AI tools for military intelligence. A crucial element of this strategy is leveraging the global open-source AI ecosystem. U.S. export controls have focused heavily on restricting China's access to advanced semiconductor hardware. However, the PLA is mitigating this by taking powerful, publicly available foundation models and adapting them for military use. Researchers at the **Academy of Military Science (AMS)** have published studies detailing their use of Meta's open-source **Llama 13B** model, which they fine-tuned on their own data to create a specialized tool for military OSINT. Similarly, a Chinese defense contractor claims to have provided a PLA unit with an OSINT model based on the powerful domestic **DeepSeek** LLM. This "open-source leapfrog" strategy allows the PLA to acquire state-of-the-art AI software capabilities without having to develop the foundational models from scratch, significantly accelerating their development cycle in the critical software layer of AI and bypassing a key chokepoint of Western policy.  
* **Cognitive Confrontation:** PLA strategists view generative AI as a revolutionary tool for **"cognitive warfare"**. PLA Major General Zeng Haiqing has noted that LLMs could be used for cognitive operations, which PLA doctrine sees as key to winning wars, sometimes without fighting. The objective is to use these tools to generate adaptive, context-specific, and highly convincing disinformation in real time. By flooding digital platforms with these narratives, the PLA aims to manipulate an adversary's perceptions, sow societal discord, erode morale, and ultimately disrupt their national decision-making processes. This represents an effort to weaponize information at an unprecedented scale and speed, making the cognitive domain a central battlefield of future conflicts.

## **Part IV: The Global Context and Future Trajectory**

China's rapid integration of artificial intelligence into its military apparatus does not occur in a vacuum. It is unfolding within a complex global environment where technology, strategy, and diplomacy are deeply intertwined. This final section synthesizes the report's findings to assess China's strategic position. It examines the stark dichotomy between Beijing's public posture on AI governance and its internal military ambitions, providing a forward-looking analysis of the trajectory, challenges, and profound strategic implications of its "intelligentization" drive for the international community.

### **Chapter 9: Governance and Global Posture \- A Dual-Track Strategy**

China has emerged as a vocal and active participant in global discussions on the governance of military AI. Through international forums like the United Nations, Beijing has projected an image of a responsible stakeholder, keen on establishing norms and guardrails for this powerful technology. However, this public diplomacy runs in sharp contrast to the unconstrained development and doctrinal ambitions evident in its internal military discourse.

#### **Public Stance on AI Governance**

Beijing has formally presented its views on the regulation of AI in the military domain through a series of position papers and initiatives.

* In December 2021, China submitted a **Position Paper on Regulating the Military Applications of Artificial Intelligence** to the UN Group of Governmental Experts on Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems. This document called for ensuring that AI remains a "tool for humanity" and advocated for strengthening human control over AI weapon systems.  
* In 2023, China launched its **Global Artificial Intelligence Governance Initiative**. In statements to the UN supporting this initiative, Chinese representatives articulated several key principles that should govern military AI :  
  * **Prudence and Responsibility (慎重负责):** States, especially major powers, should adopt a cautious and responsible attitude, avoiding the pursuit of absolute military advantage or an AI-fueled arms race.  
  * **Human-Centricity (以人为本):** Humans must remain the ultimate responsible party, ensuring that people, not machines, control weapon systems.  
  * **"Intelligent for Good" (智能向善):** The military application of AI should serve to maintain peace and must comply with International Humanitarian Law.

This public posture is designed to portray China as a constructive force in shaping global norms, aligning with international concerns about the potential dangers of autonomous warfare.

#### **Contradiction with Internal Doctrine and Development**

This carefully crafted diplomatic image is fundamentally at odds with the reality of the PLA's internal strategy, doctrine, and technical development.

* **Pursuit of Dominance:** While publicly disavowing the pursuit of "absolute military advantage," the entire corpus of PLA literature on "intelligentized warfare" is predicated on achieving exactly that. The stated goal is to leverage AI to gain "decision dominance," "seize the initiative," and develop disruptive capabilities to "design" future warfare and win decisively against a powerful adversary.  
* **Engineering of Full Autonomy:** While publicly advocating for "human-centric" control, PLA-affiliated research institutions are actively patenting and developing systems for full combat autonomy. The NUDT patent for an autonomous air combat maneuvering system (CN114706418A) and the Ordnance Science and Research Academy's patent for an autonomous ground force planning system (CN112307622B) contain no provisions for meaningful human control in their operational loops; they are designed for machine-speed, autonomous execution.  
* **Rejection of External Constraints:** Despite advancing its own governance proposals, China has pointedly refused to join Western-led frameworks that might impose more tangible or verifiable constraints. For example, Beijing has not endorsed the U.S.-led **Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of Artificial Intelligence and Autonomy**, a non-binding set of principles supported by dozens of other nations.

This pattern of behavior reveals that China's approach to AI governance is not simple hypocrisy, but a sophisticated and calculated **dual-track strategy**. The public-facing diplomatic track is designed to shape international norms in a way that could slow or constrain Western military AI development, burnish China's image as a responsible global power, and gain influence in international standard-setting bodies. Simultaneously, the internal military track proceeds with a full-speed, whole-of-nation effort to achieve technological and military superiority through the unconstrained development and integration of AI. It is a strategy designed to influence the "rules of the game" for others, while internally pursuing a path to win that game under a completely different set of rules.

### **Chapter 10: Assessment and Outlook \- Trajectory, Challenges, and Implications**

The PLA is on an unambiguous and aggressive trajectory to build a fully "intelligentized" military. This drive is a central pillar of Xi Jinping's national rejuvenation project, with key milestones for integrated development targeted for 2027 and the basic completion of modernization by 2035\. The ultimate goal is a "world-class" military by mid-century, one where intelligent networks, swarms of unmanned platforms, and AI-driven command systems are fused into a cohesive, system-of-systems warfighting architecture capable of dominating any potential adversary.

#### **Key Challenges for the PLA**

Despite the powerful state backing and apparent progress, this ambitious vision faces significant hurdles.

* **Talent:** While China graduates a vast number of STEM students, the competition for elite, world-class AI talent is fierce, not only internationally but also domestically with lucrative private sector giants like Alibaba and Tencent. The PLA must compete for the best minds.  
* **Data and Integration:** China's ability to amass vast datasets is often cited as a key advantage. However, the utility of this data for military applications is not guaranteed. Real-world combat is chaotic, complex, and characterized by incomplete and deceptive information. Training AI models on clean, well-labeled datasets is different from ensuring they perform reliably in a contested, high-stakes environment where data may be biased or manipulated.  
* **System Reliability and Trust:** PLA writings themselves acknowledge the inherent risks of AI systems, including the potential for malfunctions, the "black box" problem of not understanding how an AI reached a decision, and vulnerability to adversarial manipulation (e.g., data poisoning or adversarial AI attacks). A critical unknown is how much trust PLA commanders will actually place in these nascent and untested AI systems during a real crisis, when lives and strategic outcomes are on the line.  
* **Hardware Dependencies:** Despite a massive national push for self-sufficiency, China's AI industry remains dependent on foreign technology, particularly in the design and fabrication of the most advanced semiconductors needed for high-performance AI computing. While the "open-source leapfrog" in software mitigates this vulnerability to some extent, the underlying hardware remains a critical chokepoint that U.S. export controls are designed to exploit.

#### **Strategic Implications for the International Community**

The PLA's pursuit of intelligentized warfare has profound and far-reaching implications for global security.

* **Erosion of Traditional Military Advantage:** The PLA's focus on asymmetric capabilities, particularly swarms of low-cost, intelligent, autonomous systems, is explicitly designed to counter and neutralize the high-value, high-cost platforms—such as aircraft carriers and advanced fighter jets—that have long formed the backbone of U.S. and allied military power. This threatens to upend the decades-old military balance in the Indo-Pacific and beyond.  
* **Increased Risk of Strategic Miscalculation:** The introduction of AI-driven command systems and autonomous weapons operating at machine speed could dangerously compress decision timelines in a crisis. With algorithms potentially executing reconnaissance-strike loops in seconds, the time available for human deliberation, de-escalation, and diplomacy could shrink to near zero, dramatically increasing the risk of a rapid and unintended escalation from a tactical incident to a full-scale conflict.  
* **The Normalization of the Cognitive Battlefield:** The PLA's doctrinal embrace of AI-enabled cognitive warfare represents a significant threat that blurs the lines between peace and war. The ability to use generative AI to conduct persistent, targeted, and scalable information operations against the decision-making processes, public opinion, and societal cohesion of other nations creates a new and permanent vector for strategic competition that operates below the threshold of traditional armed conflict.

In conclusion, China's journey into military AI has been a long, deliberate, and strategically coherent endeavor. From the foundational research of the 1980s to the all-encompassing national mandate for "intelligentization" today, the objective has been consistent: to leverage technology to build a military capable of fighting and winning modern wars. The world is now witnessing the maturation of this four-decade-long effort, and its consequences will define the landscape of international security for the foreseeable future.

# **The Global Surveillance Stack: An OSINT-Based Architectural Analysis**

## **Executive Overview**

### **1.1 Mission Objective and Key Judgements**

This report provides a foundational intelligence product mapping the global surveillance infrastructure stack. Operating under strict Open-Source Intelligence (OSINT) constraints, this analysis moves beyond organizational summaries to furnish a granular, evidence-based catalog of the specific software, systems, documents, and architectures that constitute the technical underpinnings of modern state and commercial surveillance. The primary objective is to expose the layered architecture of this ecosystem, from data collection to intelligence fusion, providing a verifiable technical baseline for strategic analysis, counter-intelligence, and policy formation.  
Based on a large-scale field mapping operation of publicly available data, several key judgements have been formed:  
**Key Judgement 1: The modern surveillance apparatus is no longer a monolithic, closed-source enterprise but a distributed, symbiotic ecosystem where state agencies are key architects of open-source components.** State intelligence and defense entities, particularly within the Five Eyes (FVEY) nations, are now primary sponsors and developers of powerful, open-source surveillance tools. This strategy leverages the global developer community to accelerate innovation, establish de facto technical standards, and reduce development costs. Prominent examples include the UK Ministry of Justice's development of Splink, a scalable entity resolution framework, and the UK Defence Science and Technology Laboratory's (Dstl) creation of the SAPIENT sensor fusion architecture and the Stone Soup tracking framework. By open-sourcing these core capabilities, state actors are shaping the global technology landscape to their advantage, ensuring interoperability and proliferating their preferred methodologies.  
**Key Judgement 2: The "Smart City" concept has become a primary vehicle for deploying a dense, multi-modal sensor grid for urban surveillance, with open-source platforms providing a turnkey operating system.** The civic and commercial branding of "smart cities" masks the deployment of a pervasive surveillance infrastructure. Open-source platforms, notably Snap4City, function as centralized fusion centers that integrate vast streams of data from Internet of Things (IoT) devices, public utilities, and, most critically, commercial Video Management Systems (VMS) like Milestone XProtect. This creates a powerful, queryable, and extensible urban surveillance OS, turning public infrastructure into a data collection apparatus for state and local authorities.  
**Key Judgement 3: The surveillance collection layer has decisively shifted from a primary reliance on network-centric interception to include endpoint-centric identification through client-side fingerprinting.** While traditional network interception remains a key capability, the most dynamic area of growth is in techniques that turn a user's own device into a self-identifying beacon. Client-side browser and device fingerprinting libraries, led by the widely adopted FingerprintJS, create persistent digital identifiers by collecting and hashing a multitude of hardware and software attributes. These techniques, particularly canvas fingerprinting, bypass traditional privacy measures like cookie deletion and private browsing modes, enabling persistent tracking of individuals across the web without their explicit consent or knowledge.

### **1.2 The Five-Layer Architecture Model**

To provide a coherent framework for understanding this complex ecosystem, this report organizes its findings into a five-layer architectural model. This model represents the logical flow of data from its raw state to actionable intelligence.

1. **Collection:** This layer comprises the sensors, software, and methods used to acquire raw data. It includes network intercept platforms, physical sensors in urban environments, commercial data brokers, and client-side device fingerprinting scripts.  
2. **Processing:** This layer involves the transformation of raw data into structured and analyzable information. Key functions include video analytics, content extraction, predictive modeling, and the initial processing of signals intelligence (SIGINT).  
3. **Querying:** This layer provides the human-machine interface to access the vast repositories of collected and processed data. It is characterized by contractor-provided APIs and SDKs, as well as the legal instruments (e.g., warrants) that function as a formal query language for compelling data from private entities.  
4. **Exploitation:** This layer is where intelligence is translated into action. It includes the operational use of data for targeting, resource allocation (such as in predictive policing), and commanding autonomous systems.  
5. **Fusion:** This is the highest-level layer, where data from disparate sources is correlated and integrated to create a single, coherent intelligence picture of an entity (e.g., a person, device, or organization). This layer is dominated by entity resolution and multi-sensor fusion technologies.

A conceptual diagram of this architecture would map the most significant technologies identified in this report—such as OpenLI and FingerprintJS in the Collection layer, BriefCam and predictive policing algorithms in the Processing layer, and Splink and SAPIENT in the Fusion layer—to their respective positions in the stack. This provides a strategic, single-page overview of the entire surveillance ecosystem.

### **1.3 Scope and Methodology**

This report is strictly based on publicly available information (OSINT), including materials from public code repositories, declassified government documents, academic papers, journalistic archives, and contractor whitepapers. No intrusive methods were used. A rigorous verification playbook was employed, prioritizing the triangulation of claims across multiple independent sources and performing provenance checks on all documents and codebases. ## **The Five-Layer Surveillance Architecture**

### **2.1 The Collection Layer: Ubiquitous Sensing and Interception**

The modern surveillance architecture is founded on a "collect it all" paradigm. This is achieved not through a single method, but through a diverse and overlapping set of technical and legal mechanisms designed to ensure maximum data capture across every domain of human activity. The evidence reveals four distinct, yet complementary, collection vectors: direct network access via legally mandated systems; the deployment of a physical sensor grid through public infrastructure projects; the acquisition of commercially aggregated data from a largely unregulated market; and the use of client-side code that transforms a user's personal device into a self-identifying surveillance endpoint. This multi-pronged approach ensures comprehensive data acquisition covering digital communications, physical movement, and online behavior.

#### **2.1.1 Network & Communications Intercept**

The foundation of state surveillance remains the ability to intercept communications transiting public and private networks. This capability has evolved from traditional wiretaps to sophisticated, standards-compliant software frameworks that are increasingly available as open-source projects.

* **Lawful Intercept (LI) Systems: OpenLI**The OpenLI project stands as a confirmed, operational example of a complete, open-source Lawful Intercept system designed to meet state mandates. Its provenance is a clear case study in how national legal requirements drive the creation of globally applicable surveillance technology. OpenLI was originally developed by the University of Waikato's WAND research group in New Zealand, sponsored by a consortium of local Internet Service Providers (ISPs) seeking a cost-effective way to comply with the country's Telecommunications (Interception Capability and Security) Act. The project is now maintained and commercially supported by Searchlight Ltd, which has facilitated its adoption in multiple jurisdictions, including New Zealand, Australia, Germany, and Brazil.The system's architecture is modular and aligns with the standards defined by the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI). It consists of three core components :  
  1. **Provisioner:** A central management component that receives and distributes intercept tasks (warrants) to the collection infrastructure. It exposes a REST API for integration with an operator's existing systems.  
  2. **Collector:** The distributed data capture element, deployed at Points of Presence (PoPs) within a network. It uses high-performance packet capture libraries like libtrace to mirror and filter traffic associated with a target. It can process signaling information like RADIUS for IP sessions and SIP for VoIP calls to identify relevant data streams.  
  3. **Mediator:** The component responsible for formatting the captured Intercept Related Information (IRI, or metadata) and Content of Communication (CC) into the standardized ETSI Handover Interface (HI) format and delivering it securely to the appropriate Law Enforcement Agency (LEA).

The project is built primarily in C and depends on specialized libraries developed by the WAND group, such as libtrace for packet capture and libwandder for encoding and decoding ETSI records. The open-sourcing of OpenLI represents the commodification of lawful intercept capabilities, significantly lowering the technical and financial barriers for any state or network operator to deploy a standards-compliant mass surveillance infrastructure.

* **Telephony & VoIP Intercept**As communications shift to IP-based protocols, so too do the tools for their interception. The GitHub repository li-lib-x1x2x3, published by German VoIP provider Sipgate, provides an open-source Java implementation of the ETSI X1, X2, and X3 handover interfaces. This library allows developers to implement the necessary functions for a Network Element (NE) or Mediation and Delivery Function (MDF) to comply with lawful intercept requests for VoIP traffic. Its existence demonstrates how LI requirements are being engineered directly into the software stacks of modern telecommunications services.  
* **IMSI Catcher Detection and Principles**At the tactical level, surveillance of mobile communications often relies on IMSI catchers (also known as "Stingrays"). While the devices themselves are proprietary, their operational principles can be reverse-engineered from the defensive tools designed to detect them. The **Android IMSI-Catcher Detector (AIMSICD)** is an open-source project that attempts to identify the presence of these false base stations. Its detection methods, which include checking for cell tower information consistency, monitoring for unexpected drops in encryption, and analyzing signal strength, reveal the core techniques of an IMSI catcher attack: forcing a mobile device to connect to a malicious tower, potentially downgrading its connection to intercept communications or simply logging the unique IMSI and IMEI identifiers of all phones in a given area.

#### **2.1.2 Urban Infrastructure & Physical Sensors**

The concept of the "Smart City" has provided a convenient and publicly acceptable framework for the widespread deployment of a physical sensor grid. These initiatives merge public video surveillance with a vast array of other IoT data streams, creating a centralized platform for urban monitoring and control.

* **Smart City Platforms: Snap4CitySnap4City** is a powerful, 100% open-source smart city platform developed by the DISIT Lab at the University of Florence, Italy, and has been deployed in dozens of cities across Europe. It is designed as a comprehensive data management and application-building environment for urban services.The platform's core architecture is built upon two key open-source technologies:  
  1. **FIWARE Orion Context Broker:** This is the real-time data management backbone of the system. It uses the NGSI-LD API, a standard for context information management, to handle data from heterogeneous sources like IoT devices, sensors, and legacy systems. It allows data to be modeled as "entities" with associated properties and relationships, making it queryable in real-time.  
  2. **Km4City Ontology:** This is the semantic data model that provides structure and meaning to the ingested data. It defines a knowledge base for the city, modeling everything from the street grid and points of interest to sensors and IoT devices, eliminating data silos by mapping all information to a unified model.

The critical link for surveillance purposes is Snap4City's integration with commercial Video Management Systems (VMS). The node-red-contrib-snap4city-milestone repository provides a Node-RED connector that directly links the Snap4City platform with **Milestone XProtect**, a leading global VMS product. Milestone XProtect is a scalable system used for managing large numbers of surveillance cameras in installations ranging from small businesses to critical infrastructure like airports. This integration allows Snap4City to not only ingest live video feeds but also to receive structured events and alerts from advanced video analytics modules running on the VMS.This architecture effectively transforms a city's disparate sensor networks into a single, cohesive surveillance operating system. It allows for the fusion of video surveillance data (e.g., a person detected in a restricted area) with other data streams (e.g., public transport records, traffic sensor data) within a unified, queryable platform, enabling complex event correlation and real-time monitoring on a city-wide scale.

* **Physical Layer Hardware**Underpinning these software platforms is a layer of specialized hardware designed for robust, large-scale surveillance deployments. Companies like Versitron explicitly market fiber optic and Ethernet media converters for "public safety and government use," "smart city surveillance," and "citywide cameras". These industrial-grade components ensure the secure, high-bandwidth, and low-latency data transmission required to move vast quantities of video and sensor data from the collection points to the central processing centers.

#### **2.1.3 Digital Identity & Device Fingerprinting**

A paradigm shift in online tracking has moved collection from the network to the endpoint. Instead of intercepting data in transit, these techniques co-opt the user's own web browser or mobile device, compelling it to generate a unique and persistent identifier based on its own hardware and software characteristics. This method is highly effective and difficult to evade.

* **Browser Fingerprinting Libraries: FingerprintJSFingerprintJS** is the most prominent open-source library for browser fingerprinting, with thousands of forks and widespread use. It operates by executing client-side JavaScript that queries a wide array of browser and system attributes. The values returned from these queries are then combined and hashed to produce a stable visitorId.The more advanced, albeit older, fingerprintjs2 fork provides a detailed public list of the data sources it uses, which illustrates the depth of this technique. These sources include:  
  * **Standard Attributes:** User Agent string, screen resolution, color depth, timezone, and language settings.  
  * **Hardware & API Probing:** CPU class, platform, list of installed fonts (detected via Flash or a JS/CSS side-channel), WebGL fingerprinting (which reveals details about the graphics card and driver), and canvas fingerprinting.  
  * **Browser Configuration:** Installed plugins, support for various storage mechanisms (localStorage, sessionStorage, etc.), and the DoNotTrack setting.  
  * **Advanced Signals:** WebRTC capabilities, audio/video codec availability, and touch screen properties.

The resulting identifier is stateless and remains consistent even when users clear cookies, use private/incognito mode, or connect through a VPN. The project's ecosystem includes dedicated libraries for native Android and iOS applications, as well as adapters for easy integration with tools like Google Tag Manager, facilitating its deployment at a massive scale across the web and mobile apps.

* **Canvas Fingerprinting**A particularly powerful sub-technique is **canvas fingerprinting**. This method instructs the browser to draw a hidden graphic and text using the HTML5 \<canvas\> element. Due to subtle differences in graphics hardware, operating systems, and font rendering libraries, the exact pixel-by-pixel output of this rendering process is unique to a specific device configuration. The script then extracts the rendered image data as a Base64 string and hashes it to create a highly stable and unique fingerprint. Open-source implementations, such as one inspired by the "Picasso" paper, demonstrate the core logic of drawing randomized shapes, text, and effects to the canvas to generate this signature. This technique is a primary contributor to the high accuracy of modern fingerprinting libraries.

#### **2.1.4 Location Data Acquisition**

The collection of precise location data is a cornerstone of modern surveillance, enabling the tracking of individuals' movements and the reconstruction of their "pattern of life." This data is acquired through two parallel mechanisms: purchasing it from the commercial data market and compelling its release through legal processes.

* **Commercial Data Brokers: Fog Reveal**Fog Data Science, a company founded by former Department of Homeland Security employees, markets a service called **Fog Reveal** directly to law enforcement agencies. This service provides a web-based portal with access to a massive database of historical location data, reportedly containing billions of data points from hundreds of millions of mobile devices.The data originates from the commercial advertising ecosystem. Thousands of mobile applications collect location data from users (often under the broad permissions granted upon installation) and pass this data, along with a unique mobile advertising identifier (MAID), to a network of data brokers. Fog Data Science purchases this aggregated data and makes it searchable for police. The platform allows police to perform two key types of queries without a warrant:  
  1. **Area Search:** An officer can draw a polygon on a map and specify a time range to receive a list of all device IDs that were present in that area. This is functionally equivalent to a geofence warrant.  
  2. **Device Search:** An officer can input a specific device ID to retrieve its historical location data, allowing for the reconstruction of a person's movements over time and the identification of sensitive locations like homes, workplaces, or clinics.  
* **Legal Compulsion: Geofence Warrants**Parallel to the commercial market, law enforcement uses geofence warrants (also known as "reverse location warrants") to compel technology companies like Google to search their vast location history databases. Unlike a traditional warrant that names a suspect, a geofence warrant names a time and place.The process, revealed in court filings, typically involves a multi-step query protocol :  
  1. **Step 1 (The Geofence):** Law enforcement provides Google with the coordinates of a geographic area and a time window. Google queries its Sensorvault database and returns a list of anonymized device IDs that were detected within the geofence.  
  2. **Step 2 (The Locus):** Investigators review the anonymized data, looking for patterns of interest (e.g., devices that entered and left the area at specific times). They then request that Google provide subscriber information (e.g., name and email address) for a narrowed-down list of devices.

While this process is subject to Fourth Amendment scrutiny and requires judicial approval, the technical outcome is similar to that provided by commercial services like Fog Reveal: a list of all individuals present at a specific location at a specific time.

### **2.2 The Processing Layer: Transforming Raw Data into Intelligence**

The Processing Layer is where the immense volume of raw data from the Collection Layer is ingested, structured, and analyzed to identify patterns, detect events, and extract information of intelligence value. This layer is increasingly dominated by artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) techniques. A clear technological pipeline is evident, where advanced algorithms developed for military Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (ISR) applications are adapted and productized for commercial video analytics and domestic law enforcement tools like predictive policing. The fundamental challenge—finding a signal in a noisy, high-volume data stream—is the same, whether the sensor is a military drone or a city's CCTV camera.

#### **2.2.1 Video Analytics and Content Extraction**

Raw video footage is of limited use without automated processing. Modern video analytics platforms use computer vision to transform unstructured video into a searchable, structured database of objects, events, and activities.

* **BriefCam Platform**The **BriefCam** video analytics platform, acquired by and tightly integrated with VMS provider Milestone Systems, is a leading example of this capability. Its core innovation is the patented **VIDEO SYNOPSIS®** technology, a powerful video summarization tool. It works by detecting and extracting moving objects (e.g., people, vehicles) from hours of footage and overlaying them onto a static background from the original scene. This allows an analyst to review hours of activity in minutes by watching a condensed video where events that occurred at different times are presented simultaneously.The platform's functionality is organized into three distinct modules, demonstrating the full lifecycle of video data processing :  
  1. **REVIEW:** A forensic investigation tool that allows analysts to search recorded video across multiple cameras using a vast array of filters. Searches can be conducted based on object class (person, vehicle, animal), attributes (color of clothing, vehicle type), face recognition against a watchlist, and license plate recognition (LPR).  
  2. **RESPOND:** A real-time alerting module that allows operators to configure rules to trigger alerts for specific events as they happen. This includes line-crossing, object detection in a predefined zone, and real-time face and license plate matching.  
  3. **RESEARCH:** A business intelligence tool that aggregates video metadata over time to produce dashboards, heatmaps, and trend analyses. This can be used to monitor foot traffic, dwell times, occupancy levels, and demographic segmentation, turning surveillance data into operational insights.

The tight integration between BriefCam and Milestone XProtect is crucial. Event data, including alerts and object metadata like bounding boxes, can be sent directly from BriefCam to the VMS. This structured output can then be ingested by higher-level platforms like Snap4City, creating a seamless pipeline from raw video capture to city-wide event correlation.

#### **2.2.2 Predictive Policing Frameworks**

Predictive policing applies machine learning techniques to historical crime data to forecast where and when future crimes are most likely to occur. While once the domain of proprietary software vendors like PredPol, the core algorithms are now widely understood and implemented in open-source projects.  
Multiple repositories on GitHub demonstrate the standard methodology for building a predictive policing model. The process typically involves:

1. **Data Ingestion:** Using historical crime datasets, often sourced from public open data portals from cities like Los Angeles or Boston. These datasets contain features such as crime type, date, time, and location coordinates.  
2. **Feature Engineering:** The raw data is cleaned and transformed. Timestamps are broken down into cyclical features (e.g., hour of day, day of week), and location data is aggregated into geographic zones (e.g., districts, beats).  
3. **Model Training:** A variety of machine learning algorithms are trained on the historical data to learn the relationships between the features and the likelihood of a crime occurring. Common models include Random Forest classifiers, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and time-series models like SARIMAX (Seasonal Auto-Regressive Integrated Moving Average with eXogenous factors).  
4. **Prediction & Visualization:** The trained model is then used to predict crime "hotspots" for future time periods. The output is typically a map or dashboard that visualizes the areas with the highest predicted risk, intended to guide police resource allocation and preventative patrols.

The proliferation of these open-source frameworks demonstrates that the techniques of predictive policing are now commodified and accessible to any law enforcement agency with the data and technical capacity to implement them.

#### **2.2.3 Tactical ISR & SIGINT Processing**

The most advanced data processing capabilities reside within the military and intelligence communities, particularly for processing Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (ISR) and Signals Intelligence (SIGINT) data. While the operational frameworks are highly classified, their fundamental building blocks can be found in open-source software.  
Libraries for handling system-level signals, such as human-signals for Node.js , BlueSignals for Swift , and the native signal module in Python , provide the basic tools for intercepting and reacting to software interrupts. The SIGINT signal, commonly used for user-initiated interruptions (e.g., Ctrl-C), is a primitive form of a signal that can be intercepted and processed. These low-level capabilities form the foundation upon which more complex SIGINT collection and processing systems are built.  
Furthermore, comprehensive OSINT aggregation frameworks like the OSINT-Framework and SpiderFoot demonstrate the programmatic approach to collecting and processing vast amounts of disparate data from public sources. These tools automate the initial stages of the intelligence cycle—collection and processing—mirroring in the open-source domain the large-scale data ingestion pipelines used by state intelligence agencies.

### **2.3 The Querying Layer: Accessing and Selecting Data**

The Querying Layer acts as the critical interface between the human analyst or automated system and the massive stores of collected and processed surveillance data. It provides the tools to ask specific questions and retrieve relevant information. This layer manifests in two primary forms: sophisticated software interfaces, such as APIs and SDKs provided by defense and intelligence contractors, and formal legal instruments that function as a de facto query language for compelling data from private industry.

#### **2.3.1 Contractor APIs and SDKs**

Major government contractors that provide data fusion and analysis platforms, such as Palantir Technologies, expose their systems' capabilities through Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) and Software Development Kits (SDKs). Analyzing these tools provides direct insight into the architecture and intended functionality of the underlying platforms.

* **Palantir Foundry Platform SDK**Palantir maintains public GitHub repositories for its Python SDKs, which are designed to interact with its two main platforms, Gotham and Foundry. The foundry-platform-python SDK is particularly revealing. It provides programmatic access for users to interact with the core components of the Foundry data environment.The SDK's structure and features illuminate the platform's architecture:  
  * **Data Management:** It includes modules for interacting with datasets and managing branches, indicating a sophisticated data versioning and management system akin to Git, designed to handle large, evolving datasets in a controlled manner.  
  * **Ontology Interaction:** A key feature is the ability to interact with the Palantir "Ontology," a semantic layer that models real-world entities (e.g., people, places, events) and the relationships between them. This confirms that the platform is built around an object-centric, graph-based data model, which is essential for intelligence analysis and data fusion.  
  * **Authentication and Validation:** The SDK uses industry-standard OAuth2 client credentials for secure, programmatic access and employs the Pydantic library for runtime data validation, indicating a robust and modern software engineering approach.

By exposing these functionalities, the SDK demonstrates that Palantir's platform is engineered as a comprehensive backend for large-scale intelligence analysis, providing the tools to query, manage, and model vast quantities of interconnected data.

#### **2.3.2 Law Enforcement Interfaces**

When law enforcement agencies do not have direct access to a database, they rely on interfaces provided by private companies or on legal processes that function as a structured query protocol.

* **Fog Reveal Web Portal**The Fog Reveal service provides its law enforcement customers with a direct, user-friendly query interface. This web portal allows an officer to log in and execute queries against Fog's massive database of commercially sourced location data. The interface enables an officer to define a geographic area by drawing a shape on a map and specify a time period, effectively querying the database for "all devices at this location during this time". This provides a direct, on-demand query capability that bypasses the need for warrants.  
* **Geofence Warrant Process as a Query Protocol**The legal framework for geofence warrants functions as a formal, albeit slower, query protocol for accessing data held by technology companies. The process is not a single request but a structured, multi-step interaction that resembles a database query and refinement workflow :  
  1. **Initial Query:** The warrant, with its specified geographic and temporal boundaries, serves as the initial query submitted to the company (e.g., Google).  
  2. **Initial Result Set:** The company executes this query against its internal database (e.g., Google's Sensorvault) and returns an anonymized result set containing the device IDs that match the query parameters.  
  3. **Refined Query:** Law enforcement analyzes the anonymized data and formulates a second, more specific query, requesting subscriber and identifying information for a subset of the device IDs from the initial result set.

In this context, the legal system and the company's legal compliance department act as the "API" through which the state can execute queries against privately held data repositories.

### **2.4 The Exploitation Layer: Acting on Intelligence**

The Exploitation Layer is the ultimate destination in the surveillance pipeline, where processed and queried intelligence is translated into real-world action and effect. This is the least publicly transparent layer, as it involves operational decisions and actions. However, its functions can be clearly inferred from the stated goals and capabilities of the systems in the preceding layers. The output of the surveillance stack is not merely a report; it is a set of recommended, and in some cases automated, actions designed to shape the physical or informational environment.

#### **2.4.1 Predictive Policing and Resource Allocation**

The explicit purpose of predictive policing systems is the exploitation of intelligence derived from historical crime data. The "hotspot" maps generated by models from companies like PredPol or from open-source frameworks are not academic exercises; they are operational tools designed to direct the allocation of law enforcement resources. The "exploitation" in this context is the decision to dispatch patrol cars to a specific neighborhood at a specific time, based on the system's prediction of heightened criminal risk. The goal is to preemptively act on the intelligence to prevent crime.

#### **2.4.2 Autonomous Systems and Targeting**

The most advanced form of intelligence exploitation involves the command and control of autonomous systems. This represents a tight, and often automated, loop from sensor input to real-world effect.

* **Anduril Lattice**Anduril Industries' **Lattice** platform is an AI-powered software system designed as the command-and-control (C2) backbone for a network of autonomous assets, including sensor towers, drones, and other robotic systems. Lattice ingests and fuses data from this network of sensors to create a single, real-time operating picture of an environment.Its core function is to translate this intelligence into action. The software uses AI to autonomously detect, classify, and track objects of interest (e.g., drones, vehicles, people). It then presents this information to a human operator, providing a set of options for engagement or further monitoring. This can involve tasking an autonomous drone to intercept an intruder or cueing an effector to neutralize a threat. A confirmed example of this sensor-to-effector pipeline is the integration of Lattice with Epirus's Leonidas directed-energy microwave weapon, creating a system where Lattice's AI detects and tracks a drone, which can then be engaged by the Leonidas system. This demonstrates the full cycle of surveillance exploitation: data is collected, processed, and fused to guide a kinetic or non-kinetic response.

### **2.5 The Fusion Layer: Creating a Single Source of Truth**

The Fusion Layer is arguably the most powerful and critical component of the modern surveillance stack. Its function is to solve the fundamental "big data" problem of intelligence: disparate, noisy, incomplete, and often conflicting data records must be connected to create a single, coherent, and accurate profile of a real-world entity—be it a person, a device, an organization, or a threat. This process, known as entity resolution or data fusion, is computationally intensive and has historically been a major challenge. The evidence shows that state actors, particularly in the UK, have invested heavily in solving this problem and have subsequently open-sourced their solutions, dramatically increasing the global availability of this high-end capability.

#### **2.5.1 Probabilistic Record Linkage & Entity Resolution**

Entity resolution is the process of identifying and linking records that refer to the same entity across different datasets, especially in the absence of a common unique identifier. An intelligence agency might possess a phone number from an intercept, a device ID from a geofence, a name from a police report, and a face from CCTV; the fusion layer determines that all these data points belong to the same individual.

* **SplinkSplink** is a highly scalable, open-source Python library for probabilistic record linkage, developed and maintained by the UK Ministry of Justice (MoJ). It was created for the MoJ's "Data First" program, an initiative to link massive administrative datasets from across the UK justice system (courts, prisons, probation) to enable large-scale research and analysis.Splink's methodology is based on the well-established Fellegi-Sunter model of probabilistic record linkage. A key feature is its use of an unsupervised Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm to estimate the "match weights" for different comparisons, meaning it does not require pre-labeled training data of known matches and non-matches to build its model. This makes it highly adaptable to new and diverse datasets.The library is engineered for performance and scalability, with backends that can execute linkage jobs on Spark or AWS Athena, enabling it to link datasets containing hundreds of millions of records. Its technical sophistication is evident in its use of ComparisonLevels. This feature allows an analyst to define granular, fuzzy matching rules using SQL case statements. For example, when comparing names, one level might be an exact match, another might use the Jaro-Winkler similarity score to account for minor misspellings, and another might use Levenshtein distance to measure edit distance.The development and open-sourcing of Splink by a major government department is highly significant. It represents the transfer of a core, state-developed intelligence capability into the public domain. This user-friendly and powerful tool is now being adopted by other government agencies (such as the UK's Office for National Statistics for census data analysis) and commercial entities (such as Databricks), demonstrating a clear military/state-to-commercial technology pipeline. Other open-source entity resolution libraries exist, such as Dedupe, fastLink, and Zingg, but Splink's government provenance and focus on extreme scalability make it particularly noteworthy.

#### **2.5.2 Multi-Sensor & Multi-INT Fusion**

In the military and defense context, fusion involves combining data from multiple disparate sensors (e.g., radar, electro-optical, infrared, SIGINT) to build a comprehensive picture of the operational environment and track targets within it. The UK's Defence Science and Technology Laboratory (Dstl) has been a leader in developing and open-sourcing a complete architecture for this purpose.

* **SAPIENT (Sensing for Asset Protection with Integrated Electronic Networked Technology)SAPIENT** is an open-architecture standard developed by Dstl to enable autonomous sensor fusion. It defines a standardized interface and protocol for communication between "edge nodes" (which can be sensors or effectors) and a central "fusion node." A core principle of SAPIENT is to push processing to the edge; sensors are expected to perform local processing and transmit structured information (e.g., "a vehicle of type X was detected at location Y") rather than raw data streams. This approach promotes local autonomy and dramatically reduces network bandwidth requirements.Dstl has released the key components of this architecture on GitHub, allowing any third-party developer or defense contractor to build SAPIENT-compliant systems. These components include the SAPIENT-Proto-Files which define the message formats using Google Protocol Buffers, the Apex-SAPIENT-Middleware for message routing and validation, and a Test-Harness for verifying compliance with the standard.  
* **Stone Soup**If SAPIENT is the architectural blueprint, **Stone Soup** is the algorithmic engine that powers the fusion node. Stone Soup is an open-source Python framework, also developed by Dstl, specifically for the development and testing of target tracking and state estimation algorithms.The framework provides a highly modular toolkit with components for every stage of the tracking process, including :  
  * **Models:** Transition models (how targets move) and measurement models (how sensors perceive targets).  
  * **Filters:** Implementations of various state estimation filters, such as Kalman filters and particle filters.  
  * **Data Association:** Algorithms for correlating detections from sensors with existing tracks.  
  * **Trackers:** Components for initiating, maintaining, and deleting tracks.

Dstl actively uses Stone Soup as a platform for innovation, running competitions that challenge academia and industry to develop novel sensor management and fusion algorithms within the framework, with the results often being fed back into the open-source project.Together, SAPIENT and Stone Soup represent a complete, sophisticated, and open-source architecture for building military-grade multi-sensor ISR fusion systems. Their public release signifies a deliberate strategy by a major NATO defense organization to establish its preferred architecture as a global standard, encouraging a broad ecosystem of compatible sensors and algorithms.

## **Ecosystem and Regional Analysis**

### **3.1 Key State Actors and Technology Pipelines**

The analysis of the surveillance stack reveals a significant trend: the development of foundational surveillance technologies by Western governments, which are then released as open-source projects, leading to their global proliferation and adoption.

* **United Kingdom (FVEY):** The UK has an outsized role in this ecosystem. The Ministry of Justice's development of Splink and Dstl's creation of the SAPIENT architecture and Stone Soup framework are not isolated projects. They represent a coherent and deliberate strategy to solve internal, large-scale data fusion challenges and then open-source the solutions. This approach establishes UK-developed technologies as de facto global standards in the critical intelligence domains of entity resolution and sensor fusion, fostering an international ecosystem of compatible tools and skilled developers.  
* **New Zealand (FVEY):** The case of OpenLI demonstrates how smaller FVEY nations also play a crucial role. Driven by domestic legislative requirements for lawful intercept, New Zealand's academic and commercial sectors produced a robust, standards-compliant solution that is now being exported and adopted internationally. This shows a pattern where national surveillance mandates become the seed for globally proliferated technologies.  
* **European Union:** EU-funded academic research and collaborative projects are a major source of innovation, particularly in the smart city domain. The University of Florence's development of Snap4City, built on the FIWARE framework, has created a powerful open-source platform for urban surveillance that is now used across Europe and beyond. This highlights the role of public academic funding in building components of the surveillance stack.

### **3.2 The Contractor Ecosystem**

Private defense and intelligence contractors play a vital role in integrating these technologies into operational systems for government clients. Two distinct models are apparent.

* **Palantir:** Palantir's model focuses on providing the higher-level software layers for querying, analysis, and fusion. As evidenced by its public SDKs, Palantir's platforms like Foundry are designed to ingest and model vast quantities of data collected by other systems, providing a powerful analytical workbench for intelligence analysts. They are primarily a software and data modeling provider.  
* **Anduril Industries:** Anduril represents a more vertically integrated approach. The company builds the "full stack" of surveillance, from the physical sensor hardware (e.g., the Sentry tower) to the AI-powered command, control, and fusion software (Lattice) that operates it. This model seeks to control the entire pipeline from sensor to effector, offering a complete, end-to-end solution for autonomous surveillance and response.

### **3.3 Dual-Use Proliferation**

A defining characteristic of the modern surveillance ecosystem is the inherently dual-use nature of its core software components. Technologies developed for a specific, often benign-sounding purpose, are easily repurposed for broad surveillance and social control applications.

* FingerprintJS was developed for commercial purposes like fraud detection and website analytics, but its persistent identification capability is a perfect tool for state-level tracking of individuals online.  
* Splink was created to link anonymized administrative records for social science research within the UK justice system, but its powerful entity resolution engine is equally capable of fusing intelligence records to build profiles of dissidents or activists.  
* Snap4City is promoted as a platform for improving urban efficiency and services, but its architecture is an ideal foundation for a city-wide surveillance and control system.

The open-source distribution of these tools makes their proliferation frictionless and nearly impossible to regulate through traditional export control regimes. A powerful entity resolution framework or a lawful intercept system can be downloaded and deployed by any state, corporation, or non-state actor with the requisite technical skill, bypassing international sanctions and controls.

## **Risk and Impact Assessment**

### **5.1 Impact on Civil Liberties**

The convergence of the technologies mapped in this report presents a significant and accelerating threat to civil liberties. The combination of persistent, stateless identification (FingerprintJS), warrantless acquisition of precise location data (Fog Reveal), and the state-sponsored, open-source capability to fuse these disparate datasets at scale (Splink) fundamentally erodes the practical possibility of anonymity and privacy. This stack enables the creation of detailed "pattern of life" dossiers on individuals without any requirement for a warrant or even suspicion of criminal activity. The ability to perform retrospective area searches—identifying every person who attended a protest, visited a clinic, or met at a specific location—provides the state with an unprecedented tool for monitoring political dissent, religious activity, and personal associations.

### **5.2 Normalization of Mass Surveillance**

The packaging of these powerful surveillance capabilities under benign and appealing labels like "Smart City" serves to normalize mass surveillance and integrate it into the fabric of civic life. Platforms like Snap4City are marketed with a focus on efficiency, safety, and environmental benefits, while their core architecture is designed to ingest and fuse data from every available sensor, including city-wide video surveillance networks. By integrating surveillance into essential public services, these systems make it difficult for citizens to opt out and create a public expectation of constant monitoring. This gradual integration lowers public resistance and establishes a technical and social precedent for even more intrusive forms of data collection and analysis in the future.

### **5.3 Proliferation and Export Control Challenges**

The strategic decision by major Western defense and justice departments to develop and release foundational surveillance technologies as open-source software poses a profound challenge to international security and human rights. Tools like Splink, SAPIENT, and Stone Soup are not merely software; they are codified expertise in data fusion and intelligence analysis. By placing them on public platforms like GitHub, their proliferation becomes instantaneous, global, and virtually impossible to control through traditional export control regimes that govern hardware or proprietary software. This means that any state, regardless of its human rights record or geopolitical alignment, can freely acquire and implement military-grade sensor fusion and entity resolution capabilities, dramatically accelerating the global arms race in surveillance technology.

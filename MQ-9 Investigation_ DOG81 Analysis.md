### **4.2 The COCO Paradigm: "ISR-as-a-Service"**

The flight of N361HK is a textbook example of the Contractor-Owned, Contractor-Operated (COCO) model, a paradigm increasingly referred to as "ISR-as-a-Service". In this model, a government agency pays a company for a complete service or capability—such as a specified number of flight hours of high-definition video surveillance over a particular area—rather than buying, owning, and operating the asset itself. This approach offers significant advantages to the government client, including agility, the ability to surge capacity for specific exercises or crises without a lengthy procurement process, access to the latest technology, and a greatly reduced logistical and maintenance burden. The contractor assumes responsibility for the entire lifecycle of the mission, from maintenance and flight readiness to mission execution and data delivery.  
The U.S. military is increasingly embracing this model to fill capability gaps and accelerate the adoption of new technologies. The Army, for example, has used "ISR as a service" contracts with companies like Sierra Nevada Corporation and L3Harris to provide "bridging" capabilities with modified business jets to inform the requirements for its future HADES spy plane program. The U.S. Marine Corps' own path to acquiring the MQ-9A began with a COCO deployment contract with GA-ASI, which allowed the service to gain operational experience and validate its CONOPS before committing to a full program of record. The flight of DOG81 fits perfectly within this paradigm: a government agency, whether the DoD for an exercise or DHS for border support, can simply contract GA-ASI to fly a mission using GA-ASI's plane, pilots, and maintainers, receiving only the desired ISR data feed as the final product.

### **4.3 A History of Scrutiny: Government Audits of CBP Drone Operations**

Despite the massive and ongoing investment in this capability for border security, the CBP drone program has been the subject of multiple, highly critical audits from its own oversight bodies, including the DHS Office of Inspector General (OIG) and the Government Accountability Office (GAO). These official reports raise serious questions about the program's cost-effectiveness and operational performance.  
A landmark 2015 DHS OIG audit delivered a scathing assessment of the program's value proposition. The audit found that CBP had drastically understated the program's costs, calculating the true cost per flight hour at $12,255, more than five times higher than CBP's own figure of $2,468. This meant the program's actual cost in FY2013 was $62.5 million, not the $12 million CBP had claimed. This high cost was coupled with extremely low utilization; the drones were airborne for only about 22% of their planned hours, often grounded by weather conditions.  
Most damningly, the OIG found "no evidence" that the drone program had significantly improved border security. The audits concluded that "relatively few" apprehensions of illegal border crossers could be attributed to drone operations and that the program could not demonstrate how much it had improved border security. Even when equipped with advanced sensors like the Vehicle and Dismount Exploitation Radar (VADER), which could detect thousands of potential border crossers, the system's data led to a very low percentage of actual apprehensions, highlighting a systemic failure to convert raw intelligence into actionable results on the ground. The reports consistently criticized CBP for poor management and for failing to develop adequate metrics to even evaluate the program's effectiveness.  
This history reveals a significant contradiction between the government's procurement strategy and the program's audited performance. The federal government continues to award contracts worth hundreds of millions of dollars to GA-ASI for CBP UAS support, even as its own oversight bodies have repeatedly found the program to be cost-inefficient and operationally ineffective in that specific role. This disconnect suggests that the decisions to fund and expand the drone program may be driven by factors other than demonstrated cost-benefit analysis. These factors could include political pressure to be perceived as technologically advanced and "tough on the border," the strategic value of having these high-end ISR assets available for other national security contingencies (with border security as a peacetime justification), or the powerful and effective lobbying efforts of the manufacturer.  
Furthermore, the "ISR-as-a-Service" model, while offering flexibility, also creates a powerful feedback loop that favors the incumbent contractor. By outsourcing not just manufacturing but also the entire operational ecosystem—operations, maintenance, training, and software support—the government becomes increasingly dependent on the contractor's proprietary systems and institutional knowledge. When a new requirement or upgrade is needed, the simplest, fastest, and lowest-risk path is to award a sole-source contract to the incumbent, GA-ASI, who already controls the entire system. This very justification has been used by CBP in its procurement documents. This dynamic creates a formidable barrier to entry for potential competitors and solidifies GA-ASI's market dominance, reducing the competitive pressures that might otherwise drive down costs or spur alternative technological solutions.

3. **Surge Support or Demonstration for CBP:** While less likely to be tied directly to the military exercise, the mission could have been to augment CBP's own MQ-9 fleet during a period of heightened operational interest or to demonstrate a new capability not yet present on CBP's organic assets. This could be part of GA-ASI's ongoing efforts to sell upgrades or additional services under its large support contract with the agency.

### **5.2 The Future of Outsourced ISR: Autonomy and Proliferation**

The trends identified in this analysis are not static; they are accelerating and point toward a future ISR landscape characterized by increasing autonomy and the proliferation of expeditionary platforms, all managed through sophisticated public-private partnerships.  
The next evolution of the COCO model will almost certainly involve not just contractor-operated drones, but increasingly autonomous ones. GA-ASI's central role in the Air Force's Skyborg program and its own significant internal investment in Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning (AI/ML) are clear indicators of this trajectory. This could lead to a future of **"Autonomy-as-a-Service,"** where the government contracts not for flight hours, but for mission effects achieved by swarms of collaborative, unmanned platforms that are developed, maintained, and managed by a commercial entity.  
The development of next-generation platforms like the Gray Eagle 25M (GE-25M) and the Mojave STOL UAS points to a future of more survivable, expeditionary, and versatile systems. The ability of the Mojave to operate from short, unimproved surfaces—or even from the deck of an aircraft carrier—dramatically expands the potential use cases for these large, contractor-operated drones, moving them beyond the confines of established airbases and into austere, forward-deployed environments. This will further blur the lines between military, intelligence, and commercial operations, creating complex new challenges for oversight, accountability, and the application of international law.

### **5.3 Actionable Recommendations for Continued Monitoring**

To maintain situational awareness of these evolving capabilities and the opaque world of contractor-operated ISR, a focused, multi-pronged OSINT monitoring effort is recommended. This effort should concentrate on key digital and corporate identifiers, as well as specific geographic and contextual triggers that can provide early warning of significant developments or operations.

#### **Digital and Corporate Monitoring**

* **Track Key Identifiers:** Continuous monitoring of ADS-B data associated with N-number **N361HK** and its permanent ICAO hex code **A410BC** is essential. This monitoring should be expanded to include other known N-numbers registered to **General Atomics Aeronautical Systems, Inc.** to identify the full scope of their private fleet activity.  
* **Monitor Contract Awards:** Systematically track contract announcements on official government portals like SAM.gov and defense.gov for awards to "General Atomics Aeronautical Systems, Inc." Key awarding agencies to monitor include the Air Force Life Cycle Management Center (AFLCMC), U.S. Special Operations Command (SOCOM), the Department of Homeland Security/Customs and Border Protection (DHS/CBP), and the Army Contracting Command. Close attention should be paid to contract modifications and task orders on existing Indefinite Delivery/Indefinite Quantity (ID/IQ) vehicles, as these often signal specific new work.  
* **Review FOIA Logs:** Periodically search the publicly available Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) logs for DHS, CBP, and various DoD components. Searches for terms such as "General Atomics," "GA-ASI," "MQ-9," "Reaper," or specific N-numbers can reveal other lines of public or journalistic inquiry, and potentially uncover previously undisclosed documents or operations.

#### **Geographic and Contextual Monitoring**

* **Focus on Key Locations:** Prioritize the monitoring of airspace around GA-ASI's primary flight test facilities (**El Mirage** and **Gray Butte Field** in California) and the **Yuma Proving Ground** in Arizona. Anomalous flight activity—such as non-standard callsigns, unusual loitering patterns, or the presence of new aircraft types—originating from or operating in these areas is a high-confidence indicator of developmental or sensitive operations.  
* **Cross-Reference with Exercises and TFRs:** Any observed anomalous flight activity should be immediately cross-referenced with publicly announced major military exercises (e.g., WTI, Red Flag, Northern Edge) and the issuance of relevant FAA **Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs)**. The confluence of an anomalous flight track, a key location, and a relevant exercise or TFR provides a high degree of confidence in identifying significant, non-routine ISR activity.

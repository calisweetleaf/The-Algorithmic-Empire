# **The Gray Zone Predator: A Forensic Analysis of N361HK and the Privatization of Military ISR**

## **Section 1: Asset Deconstruction: N361HK and the DOG81 Callsign**

This section establishes the foundational facts of the case, deconstructing the aircraft's identity and the anomalous callsign to build a baseline for attribution. The analysis moves from the physical and digital signatures of the aircraft to the legal and operational frameworks that enable its unique posture, addressing its identity, flight behavior, and the callsign's significance.

### **1.1 The Airframe: A Military-Grade Asset in a Civilian Guise**

The foundation of any forensic investigation is the unambiguous identification of the asset. In this case, the aircraft is definitively identified as a General Atomics Model UHK97000-15, a variant of the ubiquitous MQ-9 Predator B, more commonly known by its operational moniker, the Reaper. Manufactured in 2015 by General Atomics Aeronautical Systems, Inc. (GA-ASI), it bears the serial number FC361 and is registered under the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) N-number N361HK. Critically, the airframe is hard-coded with the unique International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) 24-bit address (Mode S hex code) of A410BC. Unlike a registration number or tactical callsign, which can be changed, this hex code is the aircraft's permanent digital fingerprint, broadcast by its Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) transponder, allowing for unambiguous tracking across its operational lifetime.  
This aircraft is not a modified civilian platform; it is a purpose-built military system. The MQ-9A is a high-performance turboprop platform powered by a Honeywell TPE331-10 engine, boasting an endurance of over 27 hours, a service ceiling of 50,000 feet, and a substantial payload capacity of 3,850 pounds. These characteristics make it an ideal platform for persistent intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) missions.  
The legal framework under which N361HK operates is as critical to its mission as its technical capabilities. FAA records confirm the aircraft holds an "Experimental" airworthiness certificate, a classification that is central to its ability to function as a quasi-military asset under a civilian registration. This certificate exempts the aircraft from the far more stringent and restrictive type-certification processes required for standard commercial or private aircraft, granting its operator significant latitude. The certificate explicitly authorizes three broad categories of use: "Unmanned \- Research and Development," "Unmanned \- Market Survey," and "Unmanned \- Crew Training".  
This legal status is a deliberate instrument for creating deniable, "gray zone" assets. A fully-badged military aircraft, such as one with a USAF tail number, is subject to strict military regulations, command structures, and a higher degree of public and congressional scrutiny. A standard civilian aircraft, conversely, would have no legal basis to conduct military-style surveillance or operate under tactical callsigns. The "Experimental" certificate bridges this gap. It allows a military-grade airframe, owned by a private corporation (GA-ASI), to operate within the National Airspace System under FAA rules, but for purposes so broadly defined that they can easily encompass direct support to military or intelligence operations under the guise of "testing" or "demonstration." This construct creates an asset that can operate from civilian or joint-use airfields with a lower public profile than a formal military deployment, while providing a degree of administrative and political insulation for the end-user. It is a calculated strategy to create a flexible, low-profile, and administratively deniable asset that complicates attribution and traditional oversight mechanisms.

### **1.2 The Callsign: Decoding "DOG81" as Operational "Code-Switching"**

The single most important behavioral anomaly observed during the July 2025 flight was the use of the callsign DOG81. Standard FAA procedure requires civilian aircraft to use their registration number for radio identification (e.g., "November Three Six One Hotel Kilo"). Military aircraft, by contrast, use tactical callsigns composed of a word and a number (e.g., "SAVAGE 51") for operational security and to simplify communications. The use of DOG81 by a civilian-registered aircraft is a clear and deliberate deviation from standard practice, indicating that it was operating under military control or in accordance with military procedures for the duration of its mission.  
The choice of the word "DOG" is not random. It was the phonetic alphabet representation for the letter "D" in the US/UK Allied military alphabet used during World War II and the immediate postwar period, before the modern NATO standard of "Delta" was fully adopted. This historical choice strongly supports the hypothesis of a parallel to the O-1 "Bird Dog," a liaison and observation aircraft from the Korean and Vietnam eras whose mission was explicitly to "find the enemy and orbit overhead" until attack assets could be brought to bear—a mission conceptually identical to modern ISR. The numerical suffix "81" aligns with observed, though unofficial, military callsign conventions where suffixes like "81" and "91" are used as "travelling callsigns" for non-standard flights, such as ferry missions or deployments to exercises away from a unit's home base.  
The use of the DOG81 callsign represents a sophisticated act of "code-switching," allowing the asset to be simultaneously clandestine to the public and overt to its military partners. While the aircraft maintains its public-facing identity via its ADS-B broadcast of the civilian N-number, N361HK, it simultaneously adopts the tactical callsign DOG81 for operational purposes within a military exercise. This dual identity allows it to "blend in" with civilian traffic for legal and administrative purposes while seamlessly integrating into the military's command and control (C2) structure for the mission. While the civilian registration provides a layer of obfuscation to the general public, the military-style callsign serves the opposite function within the operational environment. To air traffic controllers and other military aircraft, "DOG81" immediately signals that the aircraft is operating under military procedures and discipline, which is essential for safety, deconfliction, and effective C2 in crowded and complex training airspace.

### **1.3 Flight Forensics and Data Obfuscation**

The behavioral evidence from the flight track corroborates its military-style mission. The observed flight over southern Arizona was not a simple point-to-point transit but a systematic, repetitive loitering pattern over a defined Area of Interest (AOI) at an altitude of 22,050 ft. This type of orbit, often a "racetrack" or "figure-eight" pattern, is the unequivocal signature of a surveillance mission, designed to provide persistent sensor coverage of the ground below.  
Further analysis of publicly available flight data reveals another key indicator. Commercial flight tracking services such as FlightAware and Flightradar24 show no significant, long-term historical data for N361HK or its hex code A410BC. This scarcity of data is not a failure of OSINT, but a positive finding. Aircraft engaged in sensitive military or government missions are often subject to removal requests from public tracking sites under programs like the FAA's Limiting Aircraft Data Displayed (LADD). The deliberate limitation or scrubbing of public flight data is a form of digital obfuscation that corroborates the asset's sensitive mission profile. It indicates that the operator (GA-ASI) and/or its government client has taken active steps to obscure its routine movements, a behavior pattern consistent with a clandestine or quasi-military asset, not a typical R\&D platform conducting transparent testing.

### **1.4 Initial Assessment of Operational Authority**

The synthesis of this foundational evidence—a military-grade UAV owned by a major defense contractor, operating under a permissive civilian certificate, flying a military surveillance pattern with a military-style callsign in a major military training area—points definitively to a U.S. government entity as the operational authority. The specific flight profile and timing, analyzed in the next section, point overwhelmingly toward a Department of Defense (DoD) entity as the controlling authority for this specific mission. The use of a non-standard, dynamic callsign like DOG81 is more indicative of integration into a large, complex military exercise than a standing, day-to-day law enforcement patrol by an agency like Customs and Border Protection (CBP), which would likely use a more consistent and established callsign block.

## **Section 2: The Operational Ecosystem: The Yuma Nexus and Mojave Falcon 25**

The geographic and institutional context of the DOG81 flight was not coincidental. The operation of N361HK in the airspace around Yuma, Arizona, was a highly deliberate choice, leveraging a unique and powerful convergence of military training, advanced weapons testing, and real-world border security operations. This "Yuma Nexus" creates a synergistic ecosystem where a private contractor like GA-ASI can develop, test, demonstrate, and operate its systems in close proximity to its primary government customers.

### **2.1 The Yuma Nexus: A Sandbox for the Public-Private ISR Enterprise**

The Yuma area hosts an unparalleled concentration of military and government aviation units with a particular focus on unmanned systems and advanced tactical training. This co-location of key stakeholders creates a rich operational environment that is not merely a location but a strategic, self-reinforcing ecosystem for ISR innovation, sales, and operational blending.  
The components of this ecosystem include:

* **Military Customer:** Marine Corps Air Station (MCAS) Yuma is home to Marine Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Squadron 1 (VMU-1), the "Watchdogs," who operate the MQ-9A Reaper for the Marine Corps. Yuma also hosts Marine Aviation Weapons and Tactics Squadron 1 (MAWTS-1), the Marine Corps' premier advanced tactics school, which runs the large-scale Weapons and Tactics Instructor (WTI) course twice a year.  
* **Civilian Customer:** U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) Air and Marine Operations (AMO) operates its own fleet of MQ-9 Reapers from facilities at Yuma International Airport, which shares runways with MCAS Yuma, to support its border security mission.  
* **Premier Test Facility:** The cornerstone of the nexus is the U.S. Army Yuma Proving Ground (YPG). Spanning 1,300 square miles of desert terrain and controlling nearly 2,000 square miles of restricted airspace, YPG is one of the largest and most capable military test ranges in the world.

GA-ASI has a documented, long-term, and symbiotic relationship with YPG, leveraging the facility for numerous critical test programs. These include record-setting endurance flights, testing of a Self-Protection Pod for the MQ-9, and recent live-fire demonstrations of the new Mojave Short Takeoff and Landing (STOL) UAS. The company has invested in its own permanent infrastructure, including a hangar, at the proving ground, viewing it as a core component of its R\&D enterprise.  
This nexus creates an incredibly rapid and efficient innovation loop. GA-ASI can conduct internal R\&D at its private desert facilities like El Mirage and Gray Butte, bring a new capability (e.g., a new sensor pod) to its permanent facilities at YPG for rigorous testing, and then immediately demonstrate that capability to its co-located, primary customers—the USMC and CBP—who operate the same base platform. This feedback cycle is impossible when these functions are geographically and bureaucratically separated. Furthermore, the constant presence of USMC, CBP, and contractor-operated MQ-9s creates a "noisy" air environment, providing excellent cover and plausible deniability for more sensitive missions, such as testing a classified payload or supporting another government agency.

### **2.2 Operational Context: The Mojave Falcon 25 Exercise**

The timing of the DOG81 flight in July 2025 strongly correlates with Mojave Falcon 25, a massive, multi-location Army Reserve exercise that took place from late May to mid-June 2025\. The exercise involved over 9,000 personnel from the Army Reserve and active-duty components, with more than 160 units and 2,000 vehicles. It was focused on the Army's transition from counterinsurgency to large-scale combat operations (LSCO) and complex logistics, stretching across locations including Fort Hunter Liggett, Fort Irwin, Edwards Air Force Base, and the Port of Long Beach.  
A stated key objective of Mojave Falcon 25 was to test newly fielded mission command information systems: the Joint Battle Command-Platform (JBC-P) and Command Post Computing Environment (CPCE). These systems are designed for faster communication and data sharing, forming the backbone of a modern tactical C4ISR (Command, Control, Communications, Computers, Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance) network. Such systems are useless without data to populate them. The exercise also explicitly included training to protect rear-area units from simulated threats, including *surveillance*, which implies the presence of ISR assets (either friendly or acting as opposing forces) to test these defensive measures and the C2 network's response.  
Mojave Falcon 25 provided the perfect "live fire" electronic environment to test not just a sensor, but the entire ISR "kill chain" from collection to C2 integration. A contractor-operated MQ-9 like N361HK is a flexible, low-demand asset (compared to a high-demand active-duty platform like a Global Hawk or U-2) that can provide a persistent, high-fidelity ISR feed directly into the exercise's C2 network. This allows the Army Reserve to test the full data chain: Can the sensor on DOG81 find a target? Can it pass the data securely? Can the data be ingested by CPCE/JBC-P? Can a ground unit receive it and react in a timely manner? The mission was likely less about what the drone could *see* and more about testing the *process* of seeing, sharing, and acting on intelligence within the Army's new C2 architecture. The exercise itself was the testbed, and DOG81 was a critical instrument.

## **Section 3: The Contractual and Technological Framework**

The operation of N361HK is the tangible output of a sophisticated business model and a modular technological ecosystem. This section dissects the contractual architecture that underpins the flight of DOG81 and the pod-based systems that enable its operational flexibility, directly addressing questions on contracts, dual-use patterns, and specific technology trials.

### **3.1 The "ISR-as-a-Service" Architecture**

The flight of N361HK is a textbook example of the Contractor-Owned, Contractor-Operated (COCO) model, a paradigm increasingly referred to as "ISR-as-a-Service". In this model, a government agency pays a company for a complete service or capability—such as a specified number of flight hours of high-definition video surveillance over a particular area—rather than buying, owning, and operating the asset itself. This approach offers significant advantages to the government client, including agility, the ability to surge capacity for specific exercises or crises without a lengthy procurement process, access to the latest technology, and a greatly reduced logistical and maintenance burden.  
This is not a niche model but an established acquisition strategy being used by the U.S. Army to rapidly field and test new ISR capabilities before committing to a formal program of record. The Army's use of COCO contracts with companies like Sierra Nevada Corporation and L3Harris for the ARTEMIS, ARES, and now ATHENA-S "technical demonstrator" spy planes provides direct doctrinal precedent for the type of mission flown by N361HK. These programs use contractor-owned business jets as testbeds for advanced sensors, allowing the Army to learn about new capabilities and refine requirements in a real-world operational environment.  
The relationship between GA-ASI and its government clients is vast, with a contractual web worth billions of dollars for production, sustainment, and upgrades.

### **3.2 Analysis of DARPA/DoD Contractual Links**

An exhaustive search of public contract repositories and trade journals reveals no DARPA or DoD contracts or programs linked to General Atomics that reference a "DOG series" of ISR trials. This allows for a high-confidence conclusion that "DOG" in the DOG81 callsign is a tactical designator, not a formal program name. Contracts for Title 50 (intelligence) activities are inherently classified and will not appear in public databases.  
However, unclassified documents confirm that using medium-altitude platforms as testbeds for new sensors is a standard, funded practice. A 2002 DARPA budget justification explicitly funded "sensor testbed data collects" and analysis. A 2011 USAF budget document funded a "sensor testbed" and, more specifically, a "DB-110 radar integration evaluation on a medium altitude platform". While a direct contract for the DOG81 flight is not publicly available, the mission profile is highly consistent with the activities described in these unclassified "sensor testbed" and "experimental ISR" contracts. The flight was almost certainly conducted under the broad "Unmanned \- R\&D" and "Market Survey" authorities of N361HK's experimental certificate, funded by a DoD entity as part of a larger sensor or C2 system evaluation program.

### **3.3 The Enabler of Plausible Deniability: Modular Payloads**

Modular, pod-based systems are the key physical enabler for the dual-use obfuscation strategy, allowing a single, deniable airframe to be rapidly re-roled for vastly different missions. GA-ASI and its partners have developed a suite of underwing pods that allow the mission of an MQ-9 to be rapidly altered. This makes attribution based on observing the airframe alone nearly impossible, as it forces an adversary or public observer to identify the specific payload, which is a much more difficult intelligence challenge. This modularity is the physical manifestation of the "gray zone" strategy. By creating a standardized interface for pods, GA-ASI allows the end-user to treat capabilities as "apps" for the drone. One day it can be a simple video platform for CBP; the next, with a Self-Protection Pod, it becomes a survivable asset for SOCOM; the day after, with a DB-110 pod, it is a strategic reconnaissance platform.

## **Section 4: Strategic Implications: The Gulf Surveillance Web and Future Outlook**

This section broadens the analysis to address the integration of these assets into complex operational environments like the Gulf of Mexico and projects the identified trends into the future of unmanned ISR.

### **4.1 Integration into the Gulf of Mexico Surveillance Architecture**

The dense, multi-agency operational environment in the Gulf of Mexico creates a natural "surveillance web" where a flexible, contractor-operated asset could service multiple clients, further enhancing the model's deniability and efficiency. There is already an established footprint of persistent, long-endurance UAS operations in the region. CBP operates MQ-9s out of Naval Air Station Corpus Christi, Texas, with a specific mission to provide surveillance over the Gulf of Mexico and the shared border.  
GA-ASI is also heavily invested in the maritime domain. During a 2020 DoD Advanced Battle Management System (ABMS) exercise, the company flew a King Air aircraft equipped with the MQ-9's Lynx SAR payload operating in the Gulf of Mexico, where it employed a maritime wide area surveillance mode to track a simulated vessel. The company actively markets its MQ-9B SeaGuardian variant, which is optimized for maritime missions with capabilities including a 360-degree surface search radar, an Automatic Identification System (AIS), and an anti-submarine warfare (ASW) package that can deploy and monitor sonobuoys.  
The Gulf of Mexico is a complex operational area with multiple government agencies having overlapping jurisdictions and intelligence requirements. These include CBP (counter-smuggling), the U.S. Coast Guard, and DoD's USNORTHCOM and USSOUTHCOM (homeland defense, counter-drug). Joint Interagency Task Force South (JIATF-S), a key counter-drug command located at NAS Key West, is a major consumer of regional surveillance data and coordinates detection and monitoring operations. A single GA-ASI MQ-9B SeaGuardian, flying from a location like Corpus Christi, could theoretically be tasked to collect data that serves all three interests on a single flight. It could be looking for drug smugglers for JIATF-S, monitoring a vessel of interest for NORTHCOM, and providing general maritime domain awareness for CBP. This creates a highly efficient use of the asset and makes specific mission attribution extremely difficult for an outside observer. The asset becomes part of a regional "web" rather than being tied to a single, obvious mission.

### **4.2 The "Gray Zone" Asset as Deliberate Strategy**

The evidence converges to form a clear conclusion: the combination of a civilian registration (N361HK), private corporate ownership (GA-ASI), a permissive "Experimental" certificate, the use of military-style tactical callsigns (DOG81), operation from joint-use or military facilities (Yuma), and the use of modular, mission-specific payloads is not a series of coincidences. It is a calculated, holistic strategy to create a powerful, flexible, and deniable ISR capability for the U.S. government. This model allows for the rapid testing of new technologies, provides surge capacity for exercises or crises, and enables operational support for sensitive missions, all while minimizing the public profile, bureaucratic friction, and political accountability associated with a formal military deployment.

### **4.3 Future Trajectory: From ISR-as-a-Service to Autonomy-as-a-Service**

The trends identified in this analysis are accelerating and point toward a future ISR landscape characterized by increasing autonomy and the proliferation of expeditionary platforms, all managed through sophisticated public-private partnerships. The next evolution of the COCO model will almost certainly involve not just contractor-operated drones, but increasingly autonomous ones. GA-ASI's central role in the Air Force's Skyborg Vanguard Program, which aims to develop a modular artificial intelligence "brain" for unmanned aircraft, is a clear indicator of this trajectory. The company has used its own privately-owned, jet-powered MQ-20 Avenger aircraft to test, integrate, and demonstrate the government-developed Skyborg Autonomy Core System.  
Simultaneously, the development of next-generation platforms like the Mojave STOL UAS points to a future of more survivable and expeditionary systems. The ability of the Mojave to operate from short, unimproved surfaces—or even from the deck of an aircraft carrier, as demonstrated with the UK Royal Navy—dramatically expands the potential use cases for these large, contractor-operated drones, moving them beyond the confines of established airbases and into austere, forward-deployed environments.  
The convergence of these trends points toward a future of **"Autonomy-as-a-Service."** The government may contract not just for flight hours, but for mission *effects* achieved by swarms of collaborative, semi-autonomous platforms that are developed, owned, and managed by a commercial entity like GA-ASI. This will further blur the lines between commercial and state activity and create profound new challenges for oversight, accountability, and the application of international law.

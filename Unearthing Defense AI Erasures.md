This mechanism now forms the conceptual backbone of proprietary "Cognitive Command and Control" and "High complexity planning" systems, with the career trajectory of former ISX CEO Scott Fouse serving as the primary human vector for this transfer. Second, the principles of Hierarchical Temporal Memory (HTM), particularly its use of Sparse Distributed Representations (SDRs) for unsupervised anomaly detection, have been conceptually smuggled into advanced sensor fusion and electronic warfare applications. This aligns directly with Lockheed Martin Advanced Technology Laboratories' (ATL) research into "RF Spectrum-based machine learning," where the utility of the algorithm is preserved while its biological origins are discarded to obscure its lineage. Finally, the "orphaned" memristor crossbar array technology, pioneered by HP Labs circa 2008, was not abandoned but shepherded into the classified domain. It has been resurrected under DARPA's "In-Memory AI" portfolio (FRANC, OPTIMA programs), where its origins are obscured under the designation of "anonymous legacy prototypes."  
The strategic implications of these findings are significant. The deliberate erasure of these lineages creates a "black ledger" of technological debt and capability. Adversaries and domestic oversight bodies are unable to accurately model the functionality and limitations of these advanced systems because their foundational principles are unacknowledged. This provides a significant strategic advantage in terms of operational surprise but also introduces unknown points of failure derived from the original, un-cited architectures. Understanding this hidden history is critical for accurate threat assessment, future capability development, and strategic technological forecasting.

## **II. The SOAR Lineage: From Impasse to Implementation**

### **A. Dossier: The Impasse Resolution Mechanism \- A Definitive Fingerprint**

The Soar cognitive architecture, developed from the mid-1980s onward, presents a unique model for general intelligence that extends beyond simple rule-based systems. While it utilizes a production system where if-then rules operate in parallel on a symbolic working memory, its defining characteristic—its operational fingerprint—is not the execution of known procedures but its handling of novelty, uncertainty, and conflict through a mechanism known as "impasse resolution".  
The core logic of Soar is built around a decision cycle that proposes, selects, and applies operators to transform a problem state. When the architecture's procedural knowledge is insufficient to uniquely determine the next step in this cycle, an impasse occurs. The system's response is not to halt or fail, but to automatically generate a substate. The goal of this substate is to resolve the impasse by acquiring the necessary knowledge, effectively enabling a recursive, goal-oriented problem-solving process that can decompose complex tasks and generate plans organically. This sub-goaling process is the key architectural feature under investigation.  
The specific logical triggers for this sub-goaling process can be categorized into a distinct typology, providing a clear set of logical conditions to search for in contemporary systems:

* **No-Change Impasse:** Also referred to as a State No Change (SNC) impasse, this occurs when no operators are proposed for the current state. The system lacks the knowledge of what to do next. The resulting sub-goal is to find or construct a valid operator.  
* **Tie Impasse:** This arises when multiple, equally preferable operators are proposed, and the system has no control knowledge to differentiate between them. The sub-goal is to evaluate the tied operators to select the most appropriate one.  
* **Conflict Impasse:** This occurs when the preferences for proposed operators are logically inconsistent, such as when operator A is preferred over B, and B is simultaneously preferred over A.

Crucially, this impasse-driven process is directly linked to Soar's learning mechanism, "chunking". When a substate successfully resolves an impasse, the architecture automatically creates a new production rule—a "chunk"—that summarizes the results of the sub-goaling process. This new rule allows the system to bypass the impasse in similar future situations, representing a form of explanation-based learning that continuously improves performance through experience.  
This entire logical structure—the classification of decision-making failures into specific impasse types, the automatic generation of sub-goals to resolve them, and the learning of new rules from that resolution—is not merely an archaic AI technique. It is a formal architectural blueprint for creating autonomous agents capable of robust decision-making in complex, dynamic, and uncertain environments. A modern Command and Control (C2) system for managing autonomous assets, such as drone swarms or satellite constellations, must operate under precisely these conditions. A "Tie Impasse" is directly analogous to a C2 system having multiple valid engagement options for a single target, while a "No-Change Impasse" mirrors a scenario where the system encounters a novel threat for which no pre-programmed response exists. Soar's sub-goaling process is the exact function required of a "Cognitive" C2 system, providing the mechanism to move beyond simple automation (executing a script) to true autonomy (reasoning about what to do when the script runs out). The disappearance of this powerful, formally-defined architecture from public discourse is, therefore, a strategically significant event.

### **B. Anomaly Triangulation: Tracing the Ghost**

The transfer of the Soar architecture's core principles from the academic domain into proprietary defense systems can be traced through a triangulation of corporate actions, personnel movements, and the semantic echoes of its logic in modern C4ISR program descriptions.

#### **1\. Corporate Witness Protection: The ISX Corporation Acquisition (2006)**

The primary vehicle for this transfer was Lockheed Martin's acquisition of ISX Corporation in 2006\. ISX, founded in 1988, was a highly specialized DARPA contractor focused on "military decision systems," "command and control," and "information and knowledge management". The company was a key developer of the Dynamic Analysis and Replanning Tool (DART), a seminal AI-based logistics planning system used during Operation Desert Storm that reportedly paid back DARPA's entire 30-year investment in AI research in a matter of months. ISX's expertise was highly regarded, earning it two consecutive awards as DARPA's contractor of the year—a first for any company.  
On June 30, 2006, Lockheed Martin completed its acquisition of ISX for an undisclosed sum. The stated purpose was to accelerate growth in "decision systems, distributed collaborative military operations, autonomous systems, and knowledge management". Critically, ISX was not preserved as an independent subsidiary but was fully absorbed and its business operations were placed under the management of Lockheed Martin Advanced Technology Laboratories (ATL) in Cherry Hill, New Jersey.  
This corporate maneuver is a textbook example of "Corporate Witness Protection." By completely dissolving the ISX entity into its own advanced R\&D division, Lockheed Martin effectively placed the company's intellectual property and institutional knowledge into a proprietary black box. ATL's mission is explicitly to develop and transition technologies in "autonomy and intelligence," "network-centric operations," and "cognitive computing" for clients including DARPA and various military service laboratories. This mission is a direct continuation of ISX's core business. The acquisition created an IP black hole, severing the public lineage between the foundational, DARPA-funded research that ISX specialized in and the future proprietary systems developed at ATL. Any subsequent patents or technologies derived from ISX's work would be filed under the Lockheed Martin umbrella, with no easily searchable link back to ISX or the foundational architectures, like Soar, that informed its work.

## **III. The HTM Lineage: From Neocortex to Network Sensor**

### **A. Dossier: The Spatial Pooler & SDR Algebra**

Hierarchical Temporal Memory (HTM) is an unsupervised machine learning algorithm explicitly modeled on the computational principles of the neocortex. Developed by Numenta, its primary application is anomaly detection in streaming data. The architecture's core components, the Spatial Pooler (SP) and Temporal Memory (TM), have a distinct mathematical fingerprint.  
The Spatial Pooler's function is to encode arbitrary binary input streams into Sparse Distributed Representations (SDRs). An SDR is a high-dimensional binary vector with a small, fixed percentage of active bits (1s), making it robust to noise and highly efficient for downstream processing. The SP achieves this through a process of competitive Hebbian learning. It maps similar input patterns to similar SDRs, thus preserving the topological properties of the input space while enforcing a constant sparsity.  
The Temporal Memory component then learns time-based sequences of these SDRs. By observing which SDRs consistently follow others, the TM builds a predictive model of the data stream. When an incoming pattern violates these learned temporal predictions, it is flagged as an anomaly.  
The mathematical fingerprint of the HTM learning process, particularly the SP, is well-defined and consists of three distinct phases :

1. **Overlap:** Each column in the SP computes its overlap with the current input pattern, a value proportional to the number of its connected synapses that are active.  
2. **Inhibition:** A local inhibition mechanism ensures that only a small, fixed percentage of columns with the highest overlap scores become active for any given input. This enforces the sparsity of the resulting SDR.  
3. **Learning:** The permanence values of synapses on the winning, active columns are adjusted. Synapses connected to active inputs are strengthened, while those connected to inactive inputs are weakened.

This three-step algorithm—overlap, inhibit, learn—for creating topology-preserving sparse representations from raw data streams is the unique signature of the HTM architecture.

This application space aligns perfectly with the research conducted at Lockheed Martin's ATL, specifically within its "Spectrum Operations" division. ## **IV. Hardware Hauntings: The Memristor's Second Life**

### **A. The Abandoned Prototype: HP Labs and the "Missing Circuit Element"**

The memristor, or memory resistor, was first theorized as the fourth fundamental passive circuit element by Professor Leon Chua in 1971\. For decades, it remained a mathematical curiosity. In 2006-2008, a research team at HP Labs, led by R. Stanley Williams, announced the first physical realization of the device, validating Chua's theory.  
The discovery was hailed as a potential revolution in computing. As a resistor whose resistance value changes based on the history of charge that has passed through it and retains that value when powered off, it offered a path to dense, low-power, non-volatile memory. More profoundly, by arranging memristors in a dense crossbar array, they could perform analog in-memory computing. This architecture allows for the direct execution of matrix-vector multiplications—the fundamental operation of most neural networks—within the memory array itself, promising to break the "von Neumann bottleneck" caused by shuttling data between separate processing and memory units.  
Despite this transformative potential and initial predictions of commercial devices within a few years, the technology largely disappeared from HP's public-facing commercial roadmap after approximately 2010\. This "orphaning" event created a technological void, where a publicly announced, game-changing technology with clear military applications (e.g., radiation-hard, low-power neuromorphic computing) failed to materialize commercially, seemingly entering a developmental "valley of death."

### **B. The Spectral Resurrection: DARPA's In-Memory AI Programs**

Years after the memristor's public disappearance, DARPA identified the von Neumann bottleneck as a critical impediment to deploying advanced AI on power- and size-constrained tactical platforms. In response, the agency launched a portfolio of programs aimed at developing "novel memory-centric compute topologies." Two key programs in this portfolio are Foundations Required for Novel Compute (FRANC) and Optimum Processing Technology Inside Memory Arrays (OPTIMA).  
The language used in these program announcements is a form of deliberate erasure. The FRANC program seeks to "break the traditional separation of processors and memory components" to enable "low latency compute near or inside the data storage elements". The OPTIMA program is even more specific, with a goal to develop "fast, compact, power-efficient, and scalable compute-in-memory (CIM) accelerators" based on "transistor-based MCEs \[Multiply Compute Elements\] with the footprint of a single transistor". This is a precise functional description of a memristor crossbar array, yet the term "memristor" is conspicuously absent.  
This pattern suggests a deliberate strategy. A private company (HP) develops a foundational technology with immense strategic value but which is not yet commercially viable. A government agency (DARPA) recognizes this value and effectively takes over stewardship of the concept, funding its maturation "in the dark" through targeted research at universities and specialized labs. Years later, once the surrounding technology and fabrication processes have advanced, DARPA launches new, public-facing programs like OPTIMA and FRANC to "invent" the technology anew, attributing it to "anonymous legacy prototypes" and seeding it with a fresh generation of contractors. This process launders the intellectual property, resets the clock on its development history, and ensures its trajectory is aligned with DoD priorities.

### **C. The New Custodians: The OPTIMA and FRANC Contractors**

The contractors selected for the FRANC and OPTIMA programs represent a new ecosystem built to carry this resurrected technology forward. The awardees include major industry players like IBM and Infineon, as well as leading academic research groups at Princeton University, Georgia Institute of Technology, and UCLA.  
The trail is particularly clear in the case of Princeton Professor Naveen Verma. His lab, with prior DARPA funding, developed an approach called "switched-capacitor analog in-memory computing," which directly addresses some of the analog noise and scalability issues of early memristor designs. His patents explicitly cover a "Configurable in memory computing engine" and an "Analog switched-capacitor neural network". Verma's startup, EnCharge AI, was founded to commercialize this technology and is a key partner to Princeton on the $18.6 million OPTIMA grant.  
Similarly, Professor Suman Datta at Georgia Tech is a lead researcher on the OPTIMA program. His work focuses on in-memory computing using emerging devices like ferroelectric field-effect transistors (FeFETs). He previously served as the Director of ASCENT, a multi-university microelectronics research center funded by the Semiconductor Research Corporation (SRC) and DARPA, demonstrating a long-standing and deep connection to this specific line of DARPA-funded research.  
The link between the orphaned HP Labs memristor concept and the current DARPA in-memory computing portfolio is direct and verifiable. The technical descriptions align, the strategic goals match, and the key academic researchers now leading the effort have established histories with DARPA in this domain. The "Hardware Haunting" is not a spectral anomaly; it is a fully corporeal entity that has been given a new name and a new set of custodians.

## **VI. Coda: The Logic of Erasure**

The systematic erasure of technological lineages is not a matter of poor record-keeping; it is a deliberate and rational strategy with significant benefits for the prime contractors and defense agencies involved. The logic of this erasure can be understood through three primary motivations: intellectual property capture, strategic obfuscation, and the maintenance of plausible deniability.  
First, the practice represents a highly efficient form of **intellectual property capture**. When a prime contractor like Lockheed Martin acquires a small, specialized firm like ISX—a company whose expertise was cultivated over years of DARPA funding—and then dissolves that firm into its own R\&D labs, it effectively privatizes a public investment. The foundational concepts, refined through decades of government-sponsored research, are absorbed into a proprietary ecosystem. This allows the prime to build upon that work without the encumbrances of licensing, royalties, or even simple academic attribution, maximizing its competitive advantage and return on investment.  
Second, this process enables **strategic obfuscation**. An adversary attempting to model the capabilities of a U.S. autonomous C2 system would likely start from the current public literature on AI, focusing on deep learning and reinforcement learning. If they are unaware that the system's core decision-making logic is based on a symbolic, goal-driven cognitive architecture like Soar, their models will be fundamentally flawed. They will be unable to predict how the system will behave in novel scenarios that trigger an impasse, leading them to miscalculate its resilience, adaptability, and potential courses of action. Hiding the conceptual blueprint of a system is a powerful form of information warfare, creating an ambiguity that is, in itself, a strategic asset.  
Finally, erasing these lineages provides **plausible deniability** and facilitates the "black-boxing" of advanced technologies. The public discussion that began to emerge around HP's memristor in 2010 touched on its potential for creating computers that "compute like human brains". By shepherding this technology into the classified domain and re-launching it years later under the sterile monikers of FRANC and OPTIMA, its developers avoid a complex public and ethical debate about the implications of building hardware explicitly designed for neuromorphic computation. The technology is developed and deployed before a societal consensus on its use can be formed. This erasure is not just a historical footnote; it is an active, ongoing strategy of information control that shapes the technological battlefield long before the first shot is fired.

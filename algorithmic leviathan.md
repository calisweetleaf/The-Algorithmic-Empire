Palantir's Arsenal: Gotham, Foundry, AIP, TITAN, and Project Maven in Action
Palantir's suite of software platforms is designed for deep data integration and AI-driven analysis, positioning the company as a critical technology provider for military, intelligence, and civilian government agencies.

Palantir Gotham: This platform is tailored for the defense and intelligence communities, enabling users to integrate, analyze, and visualize vast quantities of disparate data to support decision-making in complex operational environments.9 Gotham's capabilities are applied to critical military functions such as targeting, autonomous tasking of sensors (from drones to satellites), and the integration of AI into the "kill chain" to enhance situational awareness and response times.63 The platform's impact has been acknowledged at high levels, with former U.S. Secretary of Defense General James Mattis stating that Palantir "came up with ground breaking technologies that help us make better decisions in combat zones".63
Palantir Foundry: While Gotham is more defense-focused, Foundry serves a broader range of clients, including commercial enterprises and civilian government agencies. It functions as a central operating system for data.13 In a controversial application, Foundry has been used to create a "master database" by merging data from various U.S. federal agencies, including the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), Social Security Administration (SSA), and Department of Homeland Security (DHS), for purposes such as tracking immigrants and identifying fraud.12 This use case has ignited significant privacy and surveillance concerns among civil liberties advocates.65
Palantir AIP (Artificial Intelligence Platform): AIP is Palantir's solution for deploying large language models (LLMs) and other cutting-edge AI capabilities securely within defense organizations, including on private, classified networks.68 The platform emphasizes auditability and interoperability with government-owned, third-party, and open-source models.68 AIP is utilized by the U.S. military for tasks such as target acquisition, battlefield logistics, and drone coordination.69 CEO Alex Karp has been vocal about AIP's role, asserting it provides "an unfair advantage to the noble warriors of the West" and that AI itself is becoming the battlefield.69
TITAN (Tactical Intelligence Targeting Access Node): This AI-powered combat vehicle and mobile intelligence ground station is a collaborative effort, with Palantir providing the core software and AI, and partners like Northrop Grumman and Anduril contributing hardware.71 TITAN is designed to process sensor data from space, high-altitude, aerial, and terrestrial layers in real-time to support targeting and enhance situational awareness. It is considered a key component of the Pentagon's broader Joint All-Domain Command and Control (JADC2) strategy.71
Project Maven: After Google withdrew from this flagship Pentagon AI initiative due to employee protests, Palantir stepped in to continue its development.64 Project Maven aims to use AI to analyze aerial surveillance footage. The MSS Nato system, a direct descendant of Maven, now employs Palantir's AI to analyze diverse battlefield data, including satellite imagery and intelligence reports, for NATO forces, demonstrating the international adoption of this Palantir-driven capability.64
Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs): Palantir is partnering with Vatn Systems to apply its advanced software to scale the manufacturing of AUVs for national defense. This collaboration is explicitly linked to Pillar 2 of the AUKUS security pact, which focuses on advanced undersea capabilities.75

The consistent theme across Palantir's offerings is the deep integration of data from myriad sources, subjected to powerful AI-driven analytics, to provide actionable intelligence and decision support. This positions Palantir's technology at the heart of the U.S. military's and intelligence community's push for AI adoption and networked warfare concepts like JADC2, while also extending its reach into controversial domestic surveillance applications.

Roadrunner/Roadrunner-M Interceptor: This twin-turbojet Vertical Take-Off and Landing (VTOL) AAV is designed for high-speed reconnaissance and surveillance, with the Roadrunner-M variant serving as a high-explosive interceptor for ground-based air defense.21 It can loiter, identify, and kinetically engage aerial threats, or be recovered and relaunched.

Five Eyes Intelligence Sharing: Palantir and Anduril's Role with Allied Nations
The Five Eyes (FVEY) alliance, comprising the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, is a cornerstone of Western intelligence cooperation, evolving from its post-WWII signals intelligence (SIGINT) origins (UKUSA Agreement) to encompass military intelligence (MILINT), human intelligence (HUMINT), and geospatial intelligence (GEOINT).93 Both Palantir and Anduril are actively expanding their presence within this critical network, positioning their technologies as integral to allied defense and intelligence architectures.

Anduril's FVEY and Allied Contracts: Anduril has publicly acknowledged securing "multibillion-dollar deals" with the U.S. military, border security agencies, and "Five Eyes allies".96 Specific examples include Anduril Australia's $100 million contract to develop AUVs for the Royal Australian Navy 87 and Anduril UK's approximately £30 million contract with the UK Ministry of Defence to supply Altius loitering munitions to Ukraine.84 These contracts demonstrate direct engagement and technology transfer to FVEY partners.

The expansion of Palantir and Anduril into FVEY nations and their alignment with strategic pacts like AUKUS indicate that their technologies are becoming increasingly embedded in the collective defense and intelligence infrastructure of these key Western allies. This facilitates greater technological and data interoperability, which is crucial for combined operations and shared situational awareness, but also potentially concentrates critical capabilities within a narrow band of Thiel-affiliated companies.



Ethical Frontiers: Autonomous Weapons and AI in Defense
The advanced AI and autonomous capabilities developed by Palantir and Anduril place them at the center of complex ethical debates surrounding modern warfare and surveillance.

Palantir's Ethical Positioning and Controversies: Palantir CEO Alex Karp often frames the company's work as "active in defending the values of the West" 10 and providing "algorithmic supremacy".69 The company states that its AIP for Defense upholds responsible AI principles, including auditability and security.68 However, Palantir's technology has been embroiled in numerous ethical controversies. Its contracts with ICE for systems like ImmigrationOS, used to track and manage deportations, have drawn widespread condemnation for enabling what critics call a "police state" infrastructure.74 Allegations of Palantir's tools being used by the Israeli military in ways that harm civilians have also surfaced, though the company denies these specific claims.74 Furthermore, a group of former Palantir employees publicly condemned the company's work with the Trump administration, citing violations of ethical principles they believed Palantir initially stood for.97
Anduril's Philosophy on Autonomous Weapons: Founder Palmer Luckey is a vocal proponent of autonomous weapons, arguing they promote peace through deterrence and reduce risks to U.S. military personnel.21 He reframes the debate as a choice between "smart weapons and dumb weapons," contending that there is "no moral high ground to making a land mine that can't tell the difference between a school bus full of children and Russian armor".21 Anduril emphasizes that its weapons systems incorporate a human "kill switch," allowing for operator intervention.21
Critiques and Concerns Regarding Autonomous Warfare: Despite Anduril's assurances, the development and deployment of lethal autonomous weapons systems (LAWS) raise profound ethical and legal questions. The United Nations Secretary-General has described LAWS as "politically unacceptable and morally repugnant".21 Key concerns articulated by ethicists, AI researchers, and international organizations include 98:

Outsourcing Lethal Decision-Making: Transferring life-and-death decisions from humans to machines.
Lack of Nuanced Ethical Judgment: AI systems may struggle with the principles of distinction (between combatants and civilians) and proportionality (ensuring civilian harm is not excessive to military advantage) in complex battlefield scenarios.
Accountability Gap: Difficulty in assigning responsibility when an autonomous system violates international law—is it the programmer, commander, manufacturer, or state?
Algorithmic Bias: AI systems trained on biased data can produce discriminatory outcomes.
Risk of Algorithmic Escalation: Autonomous systems might misinterpret ambiguous situations and escalate conflicts unintentionally.
Lowering the Threshold for Conflict: The perceived reduction in human cost (for the deploying nation) might make countries less averse to engaging in conflict.


Department of Defense AI Strategy and Ethics: The DoD has established frameworks such as the Data, Analytics, and AI Adoption Strategy (2023) and the Responsible AI Strategy and Implementation Pathway, which emphasize principles like trust, accountability, and reliability.101 The National Security Commission on AI's Final Report (2021) also called for significant investment in AI R&D, workforce training, and the development of robust ethical guidelines.101 However, the rapid pace of technological development by companies like Palantir and Anduril, driven by their innovative R&D models and close alignment with the DoD's push for AI adoption 102, presents a continuous challenge to the practical application and enforcement of these ethical principles and legal frameworks. The very nature of their core technologies—autonomous decision-making in warfare and mass-scale surveillance—inherently pushes against established norms and often outpaces the development of adequate oversight mechanisms.

Procurement Pathways: Navigating and Shaping the Defense MarketPalantir and Anduril have demonstrated considerable acumen in navigating and, at times, shaping the complex U.S. defense procurement landscape. Their strategies involve leveraging non-traditional contracting vehicles like Other Transaction Authorities (OTAs), securing sole-source contracts where possible, and aligning with broader defense acquisition reform initiatives designed to accelerate technology adoption.

Leveraging OTAs, Sole-Source Contracts, and Other Expedited Mechanisms
The DoD's push for rapid innovation has led to increased use of flexible contracting mechanisms, which companies like Palantir and Anduril are well-positioned to exploit.

Other Transaction Authority (OTA): OTAs are designed to be hyper-streamlined contracting vehicles, largely free from the encumbering regulations of the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) and Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement (DFARS).103 This flexibility makes them attractive for innovative research and development (R&D) and prototyping projects, particularly with non-traditional defense contractors.103 The National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) for Fiscal Year 2016 permanently expanded the DoD's authority to use OTAs for "prototype" projects, defined broadly to include proofs of concept, models, pilots, and novel applications of commercial technologies.104 Key conditions for using prototype OTAs include the significant participation of a non-traditional defense contractor or substantial cost-sharing by non-government sources.104 Congress has signaled a preference for the innovation enabled by OTAs, viewing the risk of their misuse as less detrimental than "stymieing innovation".104

Palantir was awarded a $178.4 million OTA contract for the development and delivery of 10 TITAN prototypes (five advanced and five basic versions), a critical component of the Army's JADC2 efforts.72
Anduril secured a $100 million fixed-amount production OTA (HQ0883-25-9-0001) to scale its edge data integration services. Significantly, this production OTA was authorized under 10 U.S. Code § 4022(f), which allows for follow-on production contracts after a successful prototype OTA, streamlining the transition from development to fielding.105
Anduril also received a delivery order (M6785425F1010) for "Organic Precision Fires," involving amounts of $2.1 million and $1.5 million.106 While the snippet doesn't explicitly state this is an OTA, the nature of Anduril's rapid development programs often aligns with such mechanisms.


Sole-Source Contracts: These contracts are awarded without full and open competition and require robust justification, such as when only one responsible source can meet the government's needs, for follow-on production of a major system or highly specialized equipment, for unique capabilities, or in cases of unusual and compelling urgency.107

Anduril was awarded a $25.375 million sole-source delivery order (FA882024FB003) by the Office of the Secretary of Defense for its latticed mesh network communications capability. The justification for this sole-source award was its status as a Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) Phase III project, a statutory authority for non-competitive follow-on awards.108


Traditional Competitive Contracts: It is important to note that these companies also compete for and win traditional contracts. For example, Anduril's $642 million Navy contract (M67854-25-D-0003) for the Installations-Counter small Unmanned Aircraft Systems (I-CsUAS) was competitively procured, with Anduril selected from ten offerors.109 Palantir's $795 million modification to its Maven Smart System contract (W911QX-24-D-0012), bringing the total to nearly $1.3 billion, appears to be a traditional contract or an Indefinite Delivery/Indefinite Quantity (IDIQ) vehicle, rather than an OTA.111
The ability of Palantir and Anduril to effectively utilize a range of contracting mechanisms, from flexible OTAs and justified sole-source awards to winning large competitive bids, demonstrates their sophisticated understanding of the procurement system. The pathway from a prototype OTA to a potentially larger, non-competed production OTA is a particularly advantageous feature of current acquisition regulations for companies like Anduril that focus on rapid prototyping and iteration.



Palantir's FedStart and Anduril's "Product-First" Model vs. Traditional Contracting
Both companies employ business models that challenge traditional defense contracting norms, aiming to accelerate technology adoption and integrate more closely with government operations.

Palantir FedStart Program: This Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) offering is designed to significantly accelerate the federal go-to-market strategy for Palantir's partners, such as the AI company Anthropic with its Claude application.113 FedStart enables these partner companies to run their software products within Palantir's existing Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP) and DoD Impact Level (IL) accredited environments.113 This effectively lowers the often-prohibitive barriers to entry for software companies seeking to sell to the federal government by streamlining the complex and time-consuming security compliance and accreditation processes. Palantir's FedStart positions the company as a crucial enabler, and potentially a gatekeeper, for other technology firms aiming to access the federal market. This creates an ecosystem where other companies may become reliant on Palantir's accredited infrastructure, potentially giving Palantir leverage and insight into emerging technologies entering the government space.
Anduril's "Product-First" / "Defense Products Company" Model: Anduril explicitly rejects the traditional cost-plus contracting model, where companies typically wait for government Requests for Proposals (RFPs) and then build to detailed specifications.21 Instead, Anduril proactively designs, develops, and deploys systems by anticipating future military needs, absorbing the upfront R&D costs itself.24 This "build first, then sell" approach is intended to undercut larger, slower legacy contractors on price and significantly reduce execution risk for the DoD, as the government is purchasing a proven product rather than funding a development effort.24 Anduril's leadership views traditional defense procurement as overly slow, excessively bureaucratic, hardware-centric, and risk-averse, ultimately stifling the innovation needed to address modern threats.17 This disruptive business model is strategically designed to exploit these perceived weaknesses in the legacy system, leveraging speed and internal R&D to offer agile solutions that are attractive to a DoD actively seeking rapid technological advancement.



GAO Scrutiny and Defense Acquisition Reform Efforts (SWIFT, Replicator)
The procurement strategies of Palantir and Anduril operate within a dynamic landscape of ongoing defense acquisition reform efforts and oversight from bodies like the Government Accountability Office (GAO).

GAO's Role and Findings: The GAO provides crucial auditing, evaluative, and investigative services to Congress, including issuing legal decisions on bid protests related to federal contracts.115 GAO reports have consistently highlighted vulnerabilities within DoD contracting to fraud, waste, and abuse, citing weaknesses in senior leadership, the acquisition workforce, pricing practices, the selection of contracting approaches, and contract surveillance.116 These systemic issues create an environment where new, faster acquisition pathways, if not carefully managed, could introduce new risks.
Anduril's GAO Protest (B-419420): Anduril itself has engaged with the GAO, protesting an Air Force Fair Opportunity Proposal Request (FOPR) for tactical edge node support under an Advanced Battle Management System (ABMS) IDIQ contract. Anduril argued that the FOPR improperly exceeded the scope of the underlying IDIQ contracts and was unduly restrictive of competition. The GAO denied the scope argument and stated it lacked jurisdiction over the restrictiveness claim because the task order's value was below the $25 million threshold for such protests.118 This action demonstrates Anduril's willingness to use official channels to challenge procurement decisions it views as unfavorable or misaligned with contractual scope, indicating a sophisticated engagement with the regulatory aspects of defense contracting.
Pentagon Acquisition Reform Initiatives:

SWIFT (Software Fast Track) Program: This Pentagon initiative aims to accelerate the acquisition of software capabilities by using AI to replace legacy Authority to Operate (ATO) and Risk Management Framework (RMF) processes. It emphasizes third-party cybersecurity assessments and Software Bills of Materials (SBOMs) to speed up approvals.120 SWIFT is designed to facilitate the faster integration of commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) software into DoD systems.
Replicator Initiative: This DoD effort focuses on accelerating the delivery of innovative capabilities, such as all-domain attritable autonomous systems (ADA2), by leveraging non-traditional technology ecosystems and employing expeditious acquisition pathways like Commercial Solutions Openings (CSOs).121 Anduril is notably involved in Replicator through the Software Acquisition Pathway (SWP), OTAs, and CSOs for the initiative's Autonomy Collaborative Teaming (ACT) component.122 Anduril's Ghost-X drones were selected for Replicator 1.2, highlighting the company's alignment with this rapid fielding effort.80


Challenges for Non-Traditional Contractors: Despite reforms, non-traditional contractors still face significant barriers in the defense market, including cumbersome regulations, intellectual property (IP) concerns, complex cost accounting requirements, a lack of clear entry points into the DoD system, and opaque procurement processes.123 While OTAs are intended to mitigate some of these challenges, concerns remain regarding government oversight of OTAs and the handling of IP rights.124
The Pentagon's ongoing acquisition reform efforts, exemplified by SWIFT and Replicator, and the expanded use of OTAs, are creating a more conducive environment for companies like Palantir and Anduril, which are positioned as agile, non-traditional innovators. These reforms, while aiming to inject speed and cutting-edge technology into the defense enterprise, may inadvertently create new avenues for influence or capture if robust oversight mechanisms do not evolve concurrently. Palantir's FedStart program and Anduril's "product-first" model are well-suited to capitalize on these streamlined pathways. The GAO's continued scrutiny of defense contracting practices remains a critical check on these evolving procurement dynamics.

Regulations on Federal Contractor Contributions: Federal law prohibits entities that are federal government contractors (i.e., those paid with federally appropriated funds) from making contributions to federal candidates, political parties, or political committees.158 This prohibition applies directly to contributions from a partnership or an LLC that is a federal contractor, and to the personal and business funds of individuals or sole proprietors who are federal contractors.158 However, employees of federal contractors are permitted to make contributions from their personal funds, and corporations (including federal contractors) can establish Separate Segregated Funds (SSFs), commonly known as PACs, to solicit contributions from eligible individuals (e.g., stockholders, executive personnel, and their families) and make contributions to federal campaigns.159 This legal framework provides the channels through which companies like Palantir and Anduril, and their executives, can legally participate in campaign finance.

FIRRMA/CFIUS Regulatory Context: The Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act (FIRRMA) of 2018 significantly expanded the authority of the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS) to review foreign investments for national security risks.163 This includes scrutiny of non-controlling investments in U.S. businesses involved in critical technologies, critical infrastructure, or sensitive personal data (so-called "TID U.S. businesses").163 FIRRMA mandates filings for certain transactions involving TID businesses or those where a foreign government has a substantial direct or indirect interest.163 Notably, FIRRMA provides for "excepted investor" status for nationals or governments of certain allied countries, including the Five Eyes nations (Australia, Canada, New Zealand, UK), which might lead to less stringent reviews for investments from these sources.163 The increasing engagement of Palantir and Anduril with FVEY nations and their establishment of local subsidiaries (e.g., Anduril UK, Anduril Australia) occur within this evolving regulatory framework. While direct equity investment by potentially adversarial foreign state-owned entities into these sensitive U.S. defense technology companies would likely trigger intense CFIUS scrutiny, contracts and partnerships with allied governments represent a different form of foreign financial dependence and integration.

The international financial activities of the Thiel network, characterized by strategic contracts with allied governments and the establishment of foreign subsidiaries, demonstrate a clear intent to embed their technologies within the broader Western defense and intelligence architecture. The regulatory oversight provided by FIRRMA and CFIUS aims to mitigate national security risks associated with foreign investments in critical U.S. technologies, though the "excepted investor" status for close allies may create differential treatment.
